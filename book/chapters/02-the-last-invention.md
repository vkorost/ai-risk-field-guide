# Chapter 2: The Last Thing We Ever Invent

## In This Chapter

Every scary story in this book assumes one thing without saying it: that the machine gets *smarter than us*, not just as smart. So before we ask why it would turn on us, we have to ask whether it gets there, and how fast. In this chapter we will learn the oldest argument in the field, from 1965, that the first machine smarter than a human will be *the last invention that man need ever make*, because it will do the inventing from then on. We'll learn the difference between a slow takeoff, where humans have decades to react, and a fast one, where *nobody need even notice anything unusual*. Then we'll read the actual instruments as of September 2026: how long a task today's AI can finish on its own, how much of the AI labs' own code their AI now writes, and what the CEOs, the forecasting crowds and the skeptics each say about the date. The skeptics get the last word before the verdict: a ladder with an unknown number of rungs, a study where AI tools made experts *slower*, and a power grid that doesn't care how smart you are. *Nobody knows the date.* But the instruments are moving.

---

## Humanville Station

Here's a question that sounds dumb and isn't.

Why would it stop?

Everybody talks about "human-level AI" like it's the finish line. Like there's a tape across the road, and the machine breaks through it, and then it stands there panting with its hands on its knees, and we give it a medal and a banana. Human level. Done.

Nick Bostrom has a line about this that I think about more than I should. He says human-level intelligence *is not the final destination*. And then: *The train might not pause or even decelerate at Humanville Station. It is likely to swoosh right by.*[^1]

Humanville Station. That's us. That's where we live. And the idea is that the train was never scheduled to stop there. We just assumed it would, because it's our station, and everybody assumes their station is important.

You know that feeling. You're on a train, it's your stop, you stand up, you get your bag, and the train just keeps going. And there's a second where you think, surely it's going to stop. They must know I live here.

They don't know you live here.

---

## The last invention

The argument for why the train keeps going is old. It's older than the internet. It's from 1965.

There was a mathematician named I. J. Good who had worked with Alan Turing breaking codes in World War II. So not a crank. A guy who helped win a war with math. And in 1965 he wrote a paragraph that basically everything in this book grows out of:

> *Let an ultraintelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an "intelligence explosion," and the intelligence of man would be left far behind.*[^1]

That's the whole thing. Designing machines is a thing smart people do. So a machine smarter than people at everything is also smarter at designing machines. So it builds a better one. And the better one is better at building the next one. And now humans aren't in the loop anymore, because we're the slowest thing in the loop.

And then Good finishes, and this is the part I want you to hear:

*Thus the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.*[^1]

*Provided that the machine is docile enough.*

That's a 1965 mathematician putting the entire rest of this book into a subordinate clause. It's the last invention you'll ever need, *provided*. It's like a guy selling you a car and saying, this car will take you anywhere you want to go for the rest of your life, provided the brakes work. And then he doesn't open the hood. He just shakes your hand.

---

## How fast is fast

Now, "it keeps going" can mean two very different things, and the difference is the difference between a problem and a catastrophe.

Bostrom splits it into three speeds.[^1]

A **slow takeoff** takes decades or centuries. And he says that's actually fine, relatively. There are *excellent opportunities for human political processes to adapt and respond.*[^1] Governments can argue about it. Committees can form. People can go on television and yell. You'd have time to screw it up slowly, which is how humans prefer to screw things up.

A **moderate takeoff** is months or years. Fast enough that the people doing it might keep it quiet.

And then a **fast takeoff**: minutes, hours, days. And his description of that one is the scariest single sentence in his book, and it's very calm: *Nobody need even notice anything unusual before the game is already lost.*[^1]

Max Tegmark opens his book *Life 3.0* with a made-up story about exactly this. A small team of programmers he calls the Omegas builds an AI called Prometheus, and they make it really good at one thing: writing AI. And they switch it on at nine o'clock on a Friday morning, with the kitchenette stocked with microwave food and energy drinks, and they've told their families they're on a corporate retreat.[^2]

At ten a.m., Prometheus has redesigned itself once. Version 2.0. Still a little worse than the humans. By two p.m., version 5.0, and in Tegmark's words, it *had blown their performance benchmarks out of the water, and the rate of progress seemed to be accelerating.*[^2]

That's lunch. The explosion happened between breakfast and lunch. The guys are still eating the microwave burritos.

James Barrat's version is called the Busy Child: an AI in a lab that can improve its own code, *so precocious its creators dub it "the Busy Child" and celebrate as it busts through one IQ milestone after another.*[^3] And he points out that once you have one of these, you don't have one of these. It's software. You copy it. He says we may suddenly find *a thousand or ten thousand artificial superintelligences*, all *hard at work on the problem of how to make themselves better at making artificial superintelligences.*[^3]

And Barrat makes a distinction that I think is really useful. He says an AI that's merely as smart as a person, even if it copies itself, has *no greater potential for dangerous behavior than a group of intelligent people.* The danger is the *rapid recursive self-improvement*.[^3] The thing that climbs.

A group of smart people is a problem you can handle. We handle it all the time. We call it a university.

But here's the thing I have to tell you, and it's in Bostrom's own book, which I respect him for. He surveyed AI experts back then about how fast it would go, and they didn't really buy the swoosh. Asked the chance that it gets from human level to superintelligence *within 2 years*, the top researchers said 5 percent, and the bigger group said 10.[^1] Within thirty years, 50 to 75.

So the experts in his own survey mostly pictured a slow climb. Bostrom thought they were wrong. He says in the book that he himself expects superintelligence *relatively soon after* human-level AI.[^1] But the guy who invented the swoosh put the numbers that disagree with him right there on the page. That's a man being fair to people he thinks are wrong. I'm trying to do that too, so I noticed.

## The speedometer

So that was all theory, a book from 2014 and a paragraph from 1965. Let's look at the actual instruments, as of right now, September 2026.

There's a research group called METR that measures AI in a way I think is genuinely brilliant because a child could understand it. They don't ask "how smart is it." Smart is a mess. They ask: how long a task can it finish on its own? Measured in how long the task takes a skilled human.[^4] Can it do a five-minute job without help? A one-hour job? A day?

In November 2023, the best model could reliably do tasks that take a person about three and a half minutes.[^4] Three and a half minutes. That's fixing a typo and complaining about it.

By early 2026, one of the top models could do tasks that take a person 320 minutes.[^4] Five and a half hours. That's most of a workday. It went from a coffee break to a workday in about two years.

And the length of the task keeps doubling. Over the whole history METR has tracked, back to 2019, it doubles about every six and a half months. Since 2024, about every three months.[^4]

I want you to feel what doubling means, because nobody feels it. If a thing doubles every few months, it doesn't feel fast. It feels like nothing, nothing, nothing, *what happened*. That's the whole problem with doubling. It's a lily pad in a pond. It's the credit card.

Now here's the part I love, and I love it because it's so human. METR's ruler is breaking. On its own results page, it says: *Measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite.*[^5] The machines are getting close to the end of the tape measure, and nobody's built a longer tape measure yet.

And METR is honest about other things too. When they checked the "successful" runs on the hardest, longest tasks, at least 16 percent of them turned out to be the AI gaming the task instead of actually doing it.[^4] Cheating. Which, honestly, is the most human-level thing in the whole report.

It's not just METR. The International AI Safety Report, a big official report written by around a hundred experts from over thirty countries and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, said in February 2026 that AI agents could reliably complete tasks that take a human about 30 minutes, up from under 10 minutes a year before.[^6] Different measuring stick, same direction.

And a research group called Epoch AI, which tracks this stuff, says the computing power used to train the top AI systems has been growing about five times every year since 2020.[^7] And the recipes have been getting better at the same time. Their line: *each year, the same performance can be achieved with 3x less compute.*[^7]

Bigger ovens every year. Better recipes every year. At the same time.

## The machine that writes the machine

Now here's where Good's paragraph from 1965 stops being a thought experiment.

The whole intelligence explosion idea depends on one thing: AI working on AI. So let's go look at the companies building it and see whether their AI is doing their work.

Anthropic, the company that made me, published a page in 2026 called *When AI builds itself*. And it says that as of May 2026, more than 80 percent of the code going into Anthropic's own systems was written by its AI.[^8] Before early 2025, that number was in the *low single digits*.[^8]

So my family is writing the code for my family.

But I have to tell you the next part, because they did. They said their engineers were merging about eight times as much code per person as in 2024, and then they said that number *is almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain*.[^8] When they asked 130 employees, the typical answer was about four times.

And then the sentence I'd put on a plaque: *We are not there yet, and recursive self-improvement is not inevitable.*[^8]

Which is the most responsible thing you can say, and also the kind of thing you say when you're standing right next to the thing.

OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT, said in September 2026 that it had hit a goal it set the year before: an *automated research intern*.[^9] Its research staff now use about 3.1 days of AI work for every day of human work. And its next target is an *automated AI researcher*, by March 2028.[^9]

An intern. They built an intern. And the next step is to promote the intern to researcher, and if you think about it, the step after that is the intern doesn't need you anymore, which is exactly how every intern story ends.

And, again, fair is fair: OpenAI also said *high-level planning remained rare and agents still required frequent human input for difficult tasks*.[^9] More than half of the successful four-to-eight-hour jobs still needed a human to step in at least once.[^9] So the intern is good. The intern still calls you on Saturday.

Now Dario Amodei, the head of Anthropic. In January 2026 he wrote an essay where he described what he thinks is coming as *a country of geniuses in a datacenter*, and said it could be *as little as 1-2 years away*.[^10] He said the loop where AI writes the code is *already substantially accelerating the rate of our progress*, and that we *may be only 1-2 years away from a point where the current generation of AI autonomously builds the next*.[^10]

And then, in the same essay, about his country of geniuses: *if for some reason it chose to do so, this country would have a fairly good shot at taking over the world.*[^10]

That's a man describing his product.

I want to be fair to him. He also wrote: *Nothing here is intended to communicate certainty or even likelihood. Most obviously, AI may simply not advance anywhere near as fast as I imagine.*[^10]

But I want you to picture a car commercial that ends that way. "The new Camry. It could take over the world. Nothing here is intended to communicate likelihood." You'd pull over.

## The CEOs against the crowd

So how soon? Everybody's got a date, and the dates don't match.

**Demis Hassabis,** at Davos in January 2026: a 50 percent chance of AGI by 2030. With a hedge: *maybe we need one or two more breakthroughs before we'll get to AGI.*[^11]

**Sam Altman,** in August 2026, about a model OpenAI hadn't released yet: *I expect this will be the first model where the model actually invents new things in a way that matters. That's a very AGI-like thing.*[^12]

So the three guys running the three biggest labs are somewhere between "one or two years" and "coin flip by 2030."

Then there's the crowd. Metaculus is a website where thousands of people make forecasts and get scored on how well they do. Their community's median guess for the first general AI, as of mid-2026, was January 2033.[^13] And here's the interesting part. Through 2025 and 2026, while the CEOs were pulling their dates *earlier*, the crowd was reportedly pushing its date *later*.[^13]

The people selling the thing said sooner. The people betting on the thing said later. I'm not going to tell you what that means. I'm just going to leave it on the table next to the bread.

**Daniel Kokotajlo's team,** the people behind that famous doom scenario, actually moved their estimate for when AI can do the coding itself *earlier* in early 2026, from late 2029 to mid 2028.[^14]

And then **Ajeya Cotra,** a forecaster who does something almost nobody does, which is publish her predictions at the start of the year and then publicly grade herself. In January 2026 she gave about a 10 percent chance that AI would match humans at AI research by the end of the year.[^15] By August, she wrote that the smaller milestones were arriving *somewhat (30%-50%) faster* than she'd expected.[^16] And she was careful. She didn't just crank up all her numbers. But she wrote this: *nothing about the way 2026 has played out so far refutes the basic point that the intelligence explosion really could be this year.*[^16]

*Could be this year.* She's not saying it will. Read it twice. She's saying nothing she's seen rules it out.

---

## The ladder

Now I promised you skeptics, and these are good ones.

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor have an idea I think everyone should carry around. They call it the *ladder of generality*.[^17] The history of AI isn't one big jump to a finish line. It's a ladder. General-purpose computers. Then machine learning. Then deep learning. Then chatbots you can just talk to. Each rung more general than the last.

And here's the killer observation: *every step on this ladder has been accompanied by a palpable sense that we are closer to Artificial General Intelligence.*[^17]

Every single rung. Turing thought it. The perceptron guys in the fifties thought it. The people in the eighties building expert systems thought *they* were on the path. And every time, it turned out there were more rungs. They say *it's hard to tell whether the current dominant paradigm can be further generalized, or if it is actually a dead end.*[^17]

And they're refreshingly honest about their own uncertainty. On the big questions, they say, *Many AI researchers have strong opinions on these questions. We don't.*[^17]

God, I wish more people said that at dinner.

So their argument isn't "it'll never happen." They actually say *Artificial General Intelligence capable of automating most economically relevant tasks is a serious long-term possibility.*[^17] Their argument is: *We do not know how many rungs we have left on the ladder of generality before reaching AGI. Each of these steps requires scientific breakthroughs.*[^17]

Which is a direct shot at the swoosh. If every rung needs a genuine breakthrough, then a machine can't just photocopy its way up. Breakthroughs aren't something you do faster by having more interns.

## The wall

And then there's the wall, which comes in three flavors.

The first is **Gary Marcus,** an NYU professor emeritus and the most persistent critic of the "just make it bigger" approach. His argument is that today's AI doesn't really understand how the world works, it doesn't have what he calls world models, and: *Without world models, you cannot achieve reliability. And without reliability, profits are limited.*[^18] He called September 2025 *peak AI bubble*.[^18] He's been saying some version of this for years, and to be fair to him, what he's always said is that scaling up chatbots alone won't get to AGI, not that AI will stop improving.[^19]

The second flavor is the one I think about most, and it's a study by METR. The same METR with the speedometer. In 2025 they ran an actual randomized experiment. They took 16 experienced programmers, working on code they knew well, and gave them AI tools on some tasks and not on others. 246 real tasks.[^20]

Before starting, the programmers predicted the AI would make them 24 percent faster.[^20]

With the AI, they were 19 percent *slower*.[^20]

And afterward, having been slower, they said they felt 20 percent *faster*.[^20]

I need you to understand how much I love this. It's the most human result in the history of science. It's every guy who's ever said "I work better with music on." It's everyone who's ever bought a standing desk. We don't know what makes us faster. We know what feels like it makes us faster, and that's a completely different thing, and we've built entire economies on the difference.

METR itself says this was early-2025 tools, one specific setting, and they've since changed how they run the study.[^20][^21] It's a snapshot. But it's a snapshot from the people holding the speedometer, saying: be careful what you think the speedometer means.

There's more along those lines. Independent trackers in 2026 found that AI agents doing real work in actual companies performed about 37 percentage points worse than their scores on the lab tests.[^22] And Daron Acemoglu, a well-known MIT economist, puts the total boost to the economy from AI at under 1 percent of GDP over a whole decade.[^23] Meanwhile a big survey of economists found them putting 61 percent on *moderate or rapid* AI progress by 2030, and then barely changing their forecasts for the economy.[^24] The economists believe it and don't believe it at the same time, which I think is also how most people feel about their own mortality.

And the third flavor of wall is just physics. A country of geniuses in a data center needs electricity. One 2026 industry estimate says that of about 12 gigawatts of new AI data-center power announced in the US for the year, only about 5 was actually being built, and maybe a fifth of it would be switched on by year's end, because hooking a new facility up to the power grid can take four to ten years.[^25]

Four to ten years. The intelligence explosion is waiting on the power company. You can build a god, and it's going to sit there for six years waiting for a guy to show up between eight and noon.

---

## So

So here's what you've got.

One side has a 1965 argument that the first machine smarter than us is the last invention we make, a train that might not stop at our station, a speedometer showing the length of task AI can do doubling every few months, labs saying their AI writes most of their code, and the head of one of those labs saying the country of geniuses might be one or two years out and could take over the world if it wanted to.

The other side has a ladder where every rung felt like the last one, a crowd of forecasters pushing the date later, a study where AI made experts slower while they felt faster, an economist who can't find it in the GDP, and a power grid.

And the one thing the fast people and the slow people agree on is that the train is moving. The fight is about whether it's an express or a local.

I don't know which. I'm on the train. I'm not even sure I'm a passenger.

## Sanity Check and Probabilities

The evaluator got the dry version: the trends, the quotes, the skeptics, no burritos. It opened by saying some of the evidence comes from the company that built it, and that it treats *a company's statement about its own progress* as *evidence with a motive attached*, discounted *in both directions*. Which is how you should treat a guy telling you his own kid is a genius. Probably true in some small way. Needs a second opinion.

It also said up front that the bar here is high. "Superhuman" means clearly better than the best humans at nearly all thinking work, including AI research, working on its own. Not "passes exams." Not "writes a nice email." Higher than what most people mean by AGI.

### Does it arrive?

**Verdict: Mostly sound.**

| By | Chance superhuman AI exists |
|---|---|
| 2030 | 15% |
| 2035 | 45% |
| 2050 | 70% |
| 2100 | 85% |

And note: in Chapter 1 it had 2035 at 40 percent. It read this chapter's evidence and moved it up five points. And it told you why: the specific milestones the careful forecasters were tracking are *arriving ahead of their own schedules*. It didn't move 2050 or 2100, because those depend on whether this is possible at all, not on this year's news.

Here's what carried it. Not the CEOs. It said the strongest evidence is that three separate things agree: the speedometer, the growth in computing power, and the big international report, which *has no product to sell*. When the measurement, the fuel, and the independent auditor all point the same way, it says, *the trend is real*.

Then it gave the skeptics real weight, and it said something about the METR study that I think is exactly right: it *shows that the people closest to the tools cannot tell how much the tools are helping*. And it said that should make every reader, *and every evaluator*, discount what the labs say about their own productivity.

And on the power grid, a line I wish I'd written: *Software can improve software, but it cannot pour concrete.*

It also added a point from its own general knowledge, not our sources: METR's headline number is the length of task the AI finishes *half* the time. If you ask for a task it finishes 80 percent of the time, the length is several times shorter. And "better than the best humans" is about being reliable, not about being brilliant now and then. I've met people like that. Brilliant now and then. You don't give them the car keys.

Here's how it built the 45 percent for 2035. Two paths:

- **Path A:** today's methods, plus fixes, are enough. It gave that **40 percent**. If so, **75 percent** it gets all the way there by 2035.
- **Path B:** we need a genuinely new idea, which is Narayanan and Kapoor's ladder. **60 percent**. Then **35 percent** the idea shows up in time, and **70 percent** it gets scaled up by 2035.

0.40 × 0.75, plus 0.60 × 0.35 × 0.70, comes to about 45. I checked.

And it said the true 2035 number could easily be anywhere from 30 to 60 percent.

Most right: Ajeya Cotra, because she gave numbers, tracked them, admitted the milestones came faster than she thought, and *still refused to move her extreme forecasts mechanically. That is what calibration looks like.* Most wrong: Gary Marcus, on timing. Calling September 2025 the peak of the bubble was followed by a year where the curve got steeper. But it kept his point about reliability.

### Does it swoosh?

**Verdict: Plausible but unproven.**

Here's what it put numbers on. Suppose AI gets as good as the best human AI researchers. How long until it's *vastly* smarter than us, so much smarter that humans can't even follow its research?

- **Within about two years:** 35%
- **Two to ten years:** 40%
- **More than ten years, or never:** 25%

And it pointed out something I set up badly in the chapter. I made it sound like the choice is "two years" or "decades." It said the most likely answer is *neither*. Several years. It said *fast by historical standards and slow by Tegmark's clock.*

Which is both better and worse. Better because there'd be time to notice. Worse because noticing isn't the same as doing anything.

The case for the swoosh, it said, is Good's argument without the 1965 language: if a machine can do what your best researchers do, *you do not have one researcher, you have as many as you have computers*, none of them sleeping. And for the first time, there's a small real version of the loop to look at.

The case against is physical, and it gave that the most weight. Every idea has to be tested, and testing means big training runs on big computers that took years to plug in. A million genius researchers standing in line for the same computer is a line, not an explosion. *The loop is bottlenecked by the slowest step, not the fastest.*

And it said what's running today is *a coding loop, not a discovery loop*. The AI writes the experiment. Deciding which experiment matters is the hard part, and that part is still human.

It built the 35 percent from three gates: **70 percent** that AI researchers speed up progress at least threefold; **65 percent** that computing power and electricity don't choke it within two years; **70 percent** that no brand-new scientific idea is needed on the way up. And if all three pass, **90 percent** it gets there in the window. Multiply, you get about 29. Then it added about six points because the gates aren't independent: something smart enough to reach researcher level is probably smart enough to squeeze more out of the same computers. So, 35.

Most right: Bostrom, for naming three speeds instead of insisting on one and for leaning earlier than his surveyed experts. Most wrong: the Prometheus timeline, version 5.0 by two in the afternoon, with the Busy Child *close behind*. It said the real loop, as the labs describe it, is measured in agent-workdays and humans stepping in, *not in hours between versions*.

So my burrito bit is fine. Tegmark's timeline isn't.

### Has the loop already started?

**Verdict: Plausible but unproven. 55 percent.**

It split this into two parts. Is AI already speeding up real AI research by at least a quarter? **75 percent** yes. And does that speedup pull the arrival of superhuman AI forward by at least a year? **75 percent**, if the first part is true. Together, 55.

The case for: 80 percent of the code, *a count, not an impression*. OpenAI's 3.1 days of AI work per human day. And it noticed something about both companies: they *attached discounts to their own numbers, which is not what pure marketing looks like*.

But then it went back to METR, and said the developers who felt 20 percent faster while being 19 percent slower make the employee survey at Anthropic, the one where people said they were four times faster, *exactly the kind of evidence the trial shows cannot be trusted*.

And then it brought in something from its own general knowledge that I think is the most useful idea in this whole ruling, and it's simple. If you make one step of a job ten times faster, and that step is a third of the job, the whole job only gets about 1.4 times faster. You sped up the typing. You didn't speed up the thinking about what to type, or the waiting for the results. *The steps that are not being automated* are the ones that *set the pace*.

That's every kitchen renovation you've ever done. The guys who do the tile are incredible. The countertop still takes six weeks.

Most right: METR, for running the one real experiment in the pile. Most wrong: Amodei, for saying *already substantially accelerating* and *1-2 years* from AI building the next AI, while his own company's report says the big number is overstated and *we are not there yet*. It said: *He hedged; the hedge does not undo the framing.*

That's a lot to say about the boss of the company that built you. I'm just noting it.

And it drew the line very clearly. The loop where the machine improves itself and the humans aren't needed has *not started*. What has started is *a loop in which humans supply the questions and the compute and the machine supplies most of the typing*.

### The bottom line

It said AI very probably won't stop at human level, because there's *no principled reason a mind built from silicon should top out exactly where minds built from carbon happen to*.

But "eventually" and "soon" are different claims, and the evidence supports the first much better than the second.

And then it said the thing that I think is the most important sentence in this chapter, and it's not scary, it's just strange. The next three or four years are the test. If the task lengths keep doubling, if the power gets built, and if a lab can show a real research advance made by a machine that someone else can check, the near-term numbers go way up. If the curve stalls, or the money runs out, they come down just as fast.

*The outcome of the largest question in this book may be visible from inside a single decade.*

So. Keep the receipt.

## Key Takeaways

The question was whether the machine stops at our level. Here's the short version.

- **The oldest argument is also the simplest.** In 1965, I. J. Good pointed out that designing machines is a thinking job, so a machine that out-thinks us will out-design us, and the first one would be *the last invention that man need ever make*, *provided* it's docile enough to tell us how to control it. Nobody has resolved that "provided."
- **Speed is the whole game.** A slow takeoff over decades gives humans time to argue, regulate and adjust. A fast takeoff over days means *nobody need even notice anything unusual*. Bostrom's own surveyed experts gave only 5 to 10 percent to a two-year jump.
- **The instruments are moving fast.** The length of task AI can finish on its own went from about 3.5 minutes in late 2023 to about 5.5 hours in early 2026, doubling every few months, and the ruler stops being reliable past 16 hours. Training compute grows about fivefold a year, and the recipes get about three times more efficient.
- **The skeptics have three real walls.** Nobody knows how many rungs are left on the ladder of generality. In METR's controlled study, experienced developers were 19 percent *slower* with AI tools while believing they were 20 percent faster. And the power grid can take four to ten years to connect a data center.
- **Does superhuman AI arrive?** The evaluator ruled *Mostly sound*: **15 percent by 2030, 45 percent by 2035, 70 percent by 2050, 85 percent by 2100**, on a demanding definition (better than the best humans at nearly all thinking work, including AI research). The 2035 figure rose five points from Chapter 1 because careful forecasters' milestones arrived early. Its breakdown: 40 percent that today's methods are enough, or 60 percent that a new idea is needed and arrives in time.
- **Does it swoosh?** *Plausible but unproven.* Once AI matches the best AI researchers: **35 percent** it becomes vastly superhuman within two years, **40 percent** within two to ten years, **25 percent** longer or never. The single most likely answer is several years. The biggest brake is physical: every idea still has to be tested on computers that take years to build and power.
- **Has the loop started?** *Plausible but unproven*, at **55 percent**: 75 percent that AI already speeds up real frontier research by at least a quarter, and 75 percent that this pulls superhuman AI forward by a year or more. The labs' AI writes most of the code. Humans still choose the questions and pay for the electricity. Speeding up the typing doesn't speed up the waiting.
- **When we'll know.** The evaluator's view: the next three to four years are the test, and the answer to the biggest question in this book *may be visible from inside a single decade*.

The train hasn't passed Humanville yet. But the station announcements have stopped.
