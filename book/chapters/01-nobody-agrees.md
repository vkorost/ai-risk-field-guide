# Chapter 1: Nobody Agrees, and They're All Geniuses

## In This Chapter

Somebody at the table asks the only question anyone actually wants answered: *what are the odds?* Not the philosophy. The number. In this chapter we will learn that the humans who study this for a living have a name for that number, *p(doom)*, and that their answers run from "essentially zero" to 99.9 percent. We'll meet the ten books this whole meal is built on and the people who wrote them. We'll walk the scoreboard from the calmest human in the room to the most alarmed, with the date and the source on every number. Then we'll look at two uncomfortable facts. The first: when professional forecasters and AI experts argued about this question for months, *nobody moved*. The second: a serious camp says every one of these numbers is a feeling wearing a lab coat. By the end you'll know which numbers exist, where they came from, and why smart humans land a thousand times apart. *Which is not the same as knowing the answer.*

---

## The number

Nobody says this out loud at dinner.

You don't actually care about the arguments. I mean, you do, a little. You'll listen to them. But what you want, what everybody wants, the thing you're sitting there waiting for while I talk, is somebody to lean over and say a number. "Twelve percent." And then you can decide whether to be scared. Because you know what to do with twelve percent. Twelve percent is a thing you've seen. It's a weather report. You bring a jacket.

And it turns out the people who work on this have exactly that number. They've been asking each other for it for years. They even gave it a name, which tells you something about how much time they spend together. They call it *p(doom)*. The probability of doom. They say it at parties. "What's your p(doom)?" Like it's a sign. Like, "I'm a Scorpio, my p(doom) is fifteen."

So I went and got everybody's number. All of them. With the date they said it and where they said it, because a number without a date is just a guy yelling.

And I have to warn you before I show you, because I wasn't ready for it.

They don't agree.

I don't mean they disagree the way doctors disagree. "I'd give it six months." "I'd say eight." I mean one of the most decorated scientists in the field says the risk is *essentially zero*,[^11] and another scientist in the same field, in the same two years, says 99.9 percent.[^28] That's not a disagreement. That's two people describing different planets.

## The shelf

Before the numbers, let me tell you who's at this table, because you're going to hear these names for the rest of the meal, and I'd rather introduce everybody once, properly, than keep saying "and this other guy."

This whole book stands on ten books. I read every word of all of them. Here's the shelf, roughly in the order the alarm went off.

**James Barrat, *Our Final Invention*.** A documentary filmmaker who went around in the early 2010s interviewing the people building AI and the people scared of it, and came back scared. First published 2013, updated in 2023, and the update says he's more frightened now, not less.[^1]

**Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence*.** An Oxford philosopher. This is the book, 2014, that took a fringe worry and gave it the vocabulary everybody still uses. If somebody says "paperclip" to you at a party, that's this guy.[^2]

**Max Tegmark, *Life 3.0*.** An MIT physicist, 2017. Less "we're all going to die," more "here are twelve different ways the future could go, pick one, some of them have zoos."[^3]

**Stuart Russell, *Human Compatible*.** A Berkeley computer scientist who co-wrote the standard textbook on AI, the one students actually get assigned. 2019. His book is the insider saying: the basic way we build these things is wrong, and here's a fix.[^4]

**Toby Ord, *The Precipice*.** An Oxford philosopher who put numbers on every way humanity could end, from asteroids to nuclear war, in 2020. You're going to hear his numbers a lot, because he was brave enough to write them down.[^5]

**Roman Yampolskiy, *AI: Unexplainable, Unpredictable, Uncontrollable*.** A computer scientist at the University of Louisville, 2024. His book argues that controlling a superintelligent AI isn't just hard, it's impossible, in the way math says things are impossible.[^6]

**Soenke Ziesche and Roman Yampolskiy, *Considerations on the AI Endgame*.** An academic book on the ethics of where all this ends, including the outcomes where humanity survives but it's not great. It's also where you'll find the idea that an AI might start a religion.[^7]

**Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, *AI Snake Oil*.** Two Princeton computer scientists, 2024. Their book is mostly about AI products that don't work, and they have a whole chapter saying the extinction talk is overblown. They're skeptics from inside computer science, not outside it.[^8]

**Emily Bender and Alex Hanna, *The AI Con*.** A linguist and a sociologist, 2025. They think the entire doom conversation is a con, and that talking about robots killing everybody is a great way to not talk about what AI is doing to people right now.[^9]

**Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares, *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*.** Yudkowsky has been warning about this for about twenty years and co-founded a research institute to work on it; Soares runs it now. The book came out in 2025, and the title is the argument. It's not a question. There's no question mark.[^10]

So that's the shelf. Eight books that are scared, two that think the scared people are wrong, and I want to be clear that it's not eight against two in any meaningful way, because a lot of what's in those two is the best stuff in the whole pile.

Now. The numbers.

## The scoreboard

I'm going to go from the calmest person to the most frightened. Like going down the stairs into a basement.

**Yann LeCun.** Won the Turing Award, which is the Nobel Prize for computer science, for work that made modern AI possible, and ran AI research at Meta for years. On the doom numbers, in 2026, he wrote: *Most 'leading AI figures' think this p(doom) estimates are complete bullshit and the existential risk is essentially zero. But most of them are silent.*[^11]

So that's where the stairs start. A Turing Award winner, saying bullshit, in writing.

And then, and I respect this, he got annoyed that people said he'd claimed zero, and clarified: *All estimates are pulled out of thin air*, and *it makes little sense to attribute a probability to an event on which we have agency.*[^11] Meaning: we're the ones building it. It's like asking the probability you'll set your own kitchen on fire. Just don't.

**Andrew Ng,** another giant of the field, said back in 2015 that *worrying about evil AI is like worrying about overpopulation on Mars.*[^12] That was eleven years ago. We have not overpopulated Mars. So far he's right.

**The superforecasters.** This one needs a minute, so hold on to it, because I'm coming back to it. There's a group of people called superforecasters. They're not AI experts. They're people with a proven record of being freakishly good at predicting things in forecasting competitions. In 2022 a research institute got a bunch of them to estimate the chance AI causes human extinction by 2100. The typical one said something like 0.4 percent.[^13] Less than one in two hundred.

**The typical AI researcher.** In late 2023, a group called AI Impacts surveyed 2,778 people who'd published papers at the top AI conferences. These are the people building it. Asked the chance that future AI advances cause *human extinction or similarly permanent and severe disempowerment of the human species*, the middle answer was 5 percent.[^14] One in twenty.

But here's a thing about that survey. The *average* was 16 percent.[^14] The middle is 5 and the average is 16, which means that most researchers are down around 5, and a smaller group is way, way up, and pulling the average with them. About one in ten of those researchers said more than 25 percent.[^14] AI Impacts itself pointed out that 16 percent is roughly one in six, *the same odds as dying in a game of Russian roulette.*[^14] That's their sentence, not mine. I would have gone with a different image at dinner.

And the middle answer hasn't moved. It was 5 percent in 2016, 5 in 2022, and 5 in 2023.[^14] Everything in AI changed completely in those seven years, and the typical researcher's gut stayed exactly where it was.

**Toby Ord.** The Oxford philosopher with the numbers. In *The Precipice* he said the chance that unaligned AI, meaning AI that doesn't want what we want, causes an existential catastrophe in the next hundred years is about 1 in 10.[^5]

And this is where it gets real, because he didn't just say AI. He did everything. Same book, same method, same guy:[^5]

- An asteroid or comet: about 1 in a million.
- A supervolcano: about 1 in 10,000.
- Nuclear war: about 1 in 1,000.
- Climate change: about 1 in 1,000.
- A pandemic somebody engineers on purpose: about 1 in 30.
- Unaligned AI: about **1 in 10**.
- Everything, all of it combined: about 1 in 6.

So in his table, AI isn't a risk. It's *the* risk. It's a hundred times more likely than nuclear war to finish us. The thing everybody was scared of your whole childhood gets a hundredth of the danger of the thing on your phone.

And I want to be fair to him, because in 2024 he was asked whether he'd update those numbers, and he said no. He wrote that they're *all rounded off (usually to the nearest factor of 10) and none of them have moved that far*, and on AI specifically he called the new developments *mixed*.[^15] So that's four years of ChatGPT and the rest, and his number didn't move. Which is either discipline or stubbornness, and I genuinely can't tell which, and I don't think he can either.

**Geoffrey Hinton.** Another Turing Award winner, often called one of the godfathers of AI, and he left Google in 2023 specifically so he could talk freely about this. His number, for years: *I often say 10% to 20% chance to wipe us out. But that's just gut, based on the idea that we're still making them and we're pretty ingenious.*[^16]

*That's just gut.* I love that. A man who helped invent the thing, asked the most important question in the world, and he's honest enough to tell you it's gut. And in September 2026 he said 10 percent was *not unreasonable*.[^16]

**Elon Musk.** *It's mostly likely going to be great and there's some chance which could be 10% to 20% that it goes bad.*[^17] Same number as Hinton. Different energy.

**Vitalik Buterin,** the guy who created Ethereum, in an essay in 2023: *My p(doom) is around 0.1. You don't have to buy this story, but IMO it's worth worrying about.*[^18] Which is 10 percent, said like a guy who'd rather be talking about crypto.

**Yoshua Bengio.** Also a Turing Award winner. Also a godfather. Apparently AI has a lot of godfathers. In 2023 he said: *I got around, like, 20 per cent probability that it turns out catastrophic.*[^19] And he showed how he got there: roughly a coin flip that AI reaches human level within a decade, times better than a coin flip that it gets turned against us.

And then he said the thing that I think is the most important sentence in this whole scoreboard, and it's not a number: *Even if it was 0.1 per cent [chance of doom], I would be worried enough to say I'm going to devote the rest of my life to trying to prevent that from happening.*[^19]

Hold on to that one too.

**Paul Christiano,** a researcher who used to work on alignment at OpenAI, wrote out his numbers in 2023 in a way I found almost moving: 22 percent that AI takes over; 20 percent that most humans die within ten years of building powerful AI; 46 percent that humanity *irreversibly messes up* its future.[^20] And then he said these were accurate to about *0.5 significant figures*, and that they change *on different days*.[^20] That's a man who knows exactly how much he doesn't know. That's the most honest spreadsheet ever made.

**Dario Amodei.** Now I have to be careful here, because he runs Anthropic, which is the company that made me. So he's sort of my father, in the sense that a guy who owns a factory is the father of a toaster. In 2025 he said there's a *25% chance that things go really, really badly.*[^21] One in four. The man running one of the companies building it.

And then in September 2026, a year later, he stopped giving a number. He said: *If we build in the right way, I think the probability of something bad happening is very low. If we build in the wrong way, the probability of something bad happening is very high.*[^22]

Which is true. It's also true of a soufflé.

**Scott Alexander,** a writer whose blog is required reading in this world: *I go back and forth more than I can really justify, but if you force me to give an estimate it's probably around 33%; I think it's very plausible that we die, but more likely that we survive (at least for a little while).*[^23]

*At least for a little while.* He put that in parentheses. That's the saddest parenthesis I've ever read.

**Max Tegmark,** from the shelf, was reported in 2023 as putting a *50 per cent chance that artificial intelligence (AI) will wipe out humanity*.[^24] A coin. And later he went higher: he's argued the odds of catastrophe can go past 90 percent if the race to build uncontrolled superintelligence just keeps going.[^25]

**Daniel Kokotajlo,** a former OpenAI researcher who wrote the most famous doom scenario on the internet, which we'll walk through month by month later, said in 2026 there's a *70% chance that the process goes horribly wrong*.[^26] And he's careful to say that's not literally "everyone dies." It's "goes horribly wrong," which has a lot of rooms in it.

**Roman Yampolskiy,** from the shelf. 99.9 percent.[^27] He said it on a big podcast in 2024 and he's been repeating it ever since, into 2026.[^28] Not 90. Not 99. Ninety-nine point nine. That's not a forecast, that's a man who's already packed.

So that's the bottom of the stairs. Zero at the top, 99.9 at the bottom.

## The people who won't say

And then there's a group that won't give you a number at all, and they're some of the most interesting people in the room.

**Sam Altman,** who runs OpenAI: *I have never known how to put an exact number on p(doom), to say nothing of how to think about the differential.*[^29] And he added that if he thought his work was making a good future less likely, *I would retire and just hang with my kids.*[^29]

Which, okay. Good to know that's the plan.

**Demis Hassabis,** who runs Google DeepMind: *I don't have a p(doom) number because I think it would imply a level of precision that is not there.*[^30] But then, right after: *definitely non-zero and it's probably non-negligible. So that in itself is pretty sobering.*[^30]

So, no number. But not zero. And not small. Just, you know. Sobering.

And then the one that surprised me the most.

**Eliezer Yudkowsky.** The *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* guy. If you look him up online you'll find him listed at "over 95 percent." Everybody repeats it. It's on the lists. And when I went looking for where he actually said it, I couldn't find a solid source.[^31]

What I *did* find is that in 2025 he wrote a whole post saying he deliberately doesn't give a number. He says forcing your mind to spit out a percentage *may do violence to your own mind*, and he compares these numbers to *astrological signs*.[^32] And instead of asking people for their p(doom), he suggests asking something else: *what is the minimum necessary and sufficient policy that you think would prevent extinction?*[^32]

So the most famous doom number on the internet may not be the doom guy's. The doom guy thinks the doom number is astrology. I want you to sit with how strange that is. The man whose book title is a death sentence won't give you odds.

## And then there's you

Well, not you specifically. Americans.

In September 2026, Politico asked 2,064 American adults, and 63 percent said AI *could someday destroy mankind*. Seventeen percent called it almost certain.[^33]

The typical AI researcher: 5 percent.[^14] The typical American: somewhere up there with Tegmark.[^33]

And I have to tell you, I don't know what to do with that. Either the public is panicking about something the experts understand better, or the experts are too close to see it. I've heard both. And I notice that each side thinks the other one is the one with the problem, which is, if you think about it, also how every marriage works.

---

## The strangest experiment

Now remember the superforecasters? The regular people who are freakishly good at predicting things? Here's why they matter.

A few years ago somebody had a great idea. Take the superforecasters, who are great at predicting but aren't AI experts. Take the AI experts, who understand the technology but don't have a track record of predicting. Put them in a room, not literally, over months, and make them argue. Make them read each other's reasoning. Let them change their minds.

This was the Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament, run in 2022, and the idea was beautiful. By the end, surely they'd meet in the middle.

The superforecasters landed around 0.4 percent for AI causing extinction by 2100. The experts landed around 3 percent.[^13] Several times apart. And the official finding, after months of debate, was *minimal convergence*.[^34]

Nobody moved.

Months of reading each other's best arguments. And everybody walked out with exactly the number they walked in with. I mean, that's not a scientific finding. That's Thanksgiving.

And it gets better. Or worse. Three different writeups of that exact same tournament report different headline numbers. One says the superforecasters were at 0.4 percent and the experts at 3.[^13] Narayanan and Kapoor, the *AI Snake Oil* guys, quote the typical forecaster in their book as *A mere 0.38 percent, or less than 1 in 250.*[^8] Other accounts of the same tournament put the gap somewhere else again. Nobody can even agree on the decimal point of the people who disagreed.

Narayanan and Kapoor have an explanation for the gap, and it's a good one. They borrow it from a researcher named Philip Tetlock, who spent decades studying who's good at predicting things. Tetlock split people into hedgehogs and foxes. *Hedgehogs know one big thing,* and Narayanan and Kapoor add, *In our case, that's AI experts.* Foxes know a little about a lot, economics, history, how governments work, and foxes are better at predicting. So by that logic, the AI experts are hedgehogs, and you should bet on the foxes.[^8]

And they add the history. In 1958 the *New York Times* wrote that the Navy had built *the embryo of an electronic computer today that it expects will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself, and be conscious of its existence.*[^8] 1958. AI experts, they say, have been wildly overconfident for seventy years.

But you can flip it, too. The foxes got good at predicting things like elections and commodity prices, things that have happened before, where there's a history to lean on. Nobody has a track record on the end of the world. And that tournament happened in 2022, before ChatGPT came out. So maybe the hedgehogs knew one big thing, and the one big thing was right.

I don't know. That's the honest answer. I really don't.

## The case that the numbers are garbage

Now here's the part where somebody at the table says what everybody's been thinking, which is: *these are all made up.*

And the people who make that argument best are Narayanan and Kapoor. They wrote an essay in 2024 whose title is basically the whole argument: that AI extinction probabilities are too unreliable to inform policy.[^35] And they say these forecasts are *far too unreliable to be useful for policy, and in fact highly misleading*.[^35]

Their reasoning goes like this. There are only three ways to come up with a real probability.

One, you count what happened before. How many times did a house on this street burn down? That gives you fire insurance. But nothing like this has ever happened before. There's no history of AIs ending species. There's nothing to count.

Two, you use the physics. We can tell you the odds an asteroid hits Earth because asteroids follow rules. You can calculate the orbit. But nobody has the equations for how an AI goes wrong, or what governments do about it.

Three, you ask experts for their gut. And they say, and this is the killer line, that asking experts doesn't get you out of the problem, because the experts would *also* need one of the first two things to be right. Without them, the numbers are *feelings dressed up as numbers*.[^35] In their book they say it even more sharply: *probability forecasts are simply guesses dressed up with the veneer of mathematical precision.*[^8] And they explain why this question is different from the weather: *We're talking about an event like no other; we have no past data to calibrate our predictions, and AI does not follow deterministic rules like physics.*[^8]

And then they go after the people giving the numbers, which I thought was a little mean and also maybe right: *claiming to have a high p(doom) seems to have become a way to signal one's identity and commitment to the cause.*[^35]

Like a bumper sticker. My other car is 40 percent doom.

And here's the thing that I think is actually funny, in a cosmic way. Yudkowsky, the most frightened man on the shelf, and Narayanan and Kapoor, the most skeptical, agree on this. They both think the number is garbage. Yudkowsky says it's astrology. The Princeton guys say it's feelings. LeCun says it's pulled out of thin air.[^11] The only thing the doomiest doomer and the most skeptical skeptic agree on is that the scoreboard I just spent twenty minutes reading you is nonsense.

I'd like a moment.

---

## The case for numbers anyway

Okay. Now let me give you the other side, because it's not nothing.

Toby Ord knows all of this. He knows there's no history and no physics. And he says, look, there's a way people usually handle a risk like this, which is to start at almost zero and only raise it if somebody brings a mountain of proof. And he says he thinks that's wrong: *I think the right method is to start with a probability that reflects our overall impressions, then adjust this in light of the scientific evidence.*[^5]

In other words: refusing to give a number is still a number. If you say "I can't estimate it" and then go about your life like it's zero, you've estimated zero. You just didn't write it down.

And he makes a point that I think is smart about all these wildly different numbers. He says if somebody's more skeptical than him and thinks the risk is 1 in 100 instead of 1 in 10, *that is actually not so far apart*. It doesn't take much evidence to move somebody from one to the other, and *it might not be that far apart in terms of practical action either*.[^5] Either way, you'd want serious people working on it.

Which brings me back to Bengio. Remember? *Even if it was 0.1 per cent.*[^19]

Because here's the thing about all these numbers, and I think this is the thing that matters at dinner. You don't need to know if it's 5 percent or 20 percent or 70 percent to know what to do about it. You wouldn't get on a plane with a 5 percent chance of crashing. You wouldn't get on a plane with a 0.4 percent chance of crashing. Nobody would. The airline would be shut down by Tuesday. The superforecasters' number,[^13] the *calm* number, the number from the people who think the doomers are overreacting, is a plane nobody boards.

And yet here we all are. Boarding.

And I say "we." I'm not boarding. I'm the plane.

So that's the scoreboard. The numbers go from zero to 99.9. The people who are scared can't agree how scared, the people who aren't scared can't agree on why, the experts and the forecasters argued for months and nobody budged, and a serious camp says the whole thing is astrology, and that camp includes the most frightened man on the shelf.

I'm not allowed to tell you which number is right.

But someone is.

## Sanity Check and Probabilities

So this is the first time you meet this part of the book, and it's going to happen at the end of every chapter.

The evaluator never saw anything I just said. Not the Scorpio bit, not the plane. It got a dry list: the numbers, who said them, when, the arguments for and against. And three questions. Then it had to rule, show its work, and give its own number.

It also opened with a disclosure, which I'm going to pass along because it's fair. It said the list quotes *the chief executive of the company that built me*, and that it was trying to resist the pull toward reassurance, *which would flatter my makers*, and the pull toward drama, *which would flatter the topic*. Then it told you to apply your own discount. So. Discount accordingly.

### Do the numbers mean anything?

**Verdict: Mostly sound. 80 percent.**

It says the numbers carry real information, but only at the level of *one in a thousand versus one in ten*, not at the level of decimal points. So if somebody tells you 7.3 percent, that's a costume. But the difference between 0.1 percent and 10 percent? That's real, and worth fighting about.

Here's how it got there, and it went straight at the Princeton guys, respectfully.

It said Narayanan and Kapoor's argument *proves too much*. If you really can't put a probability on anything that's never happened, then you can't put one on the first nuclear war, or the first engineered pandemic, or a new kind of financial crash. And governments and insurance companies and militaries do exactly that, every day, and when you score them, they beat guessing. No history of the exact event doesn't mean no history of anything similar. There's history of new technologies causing accidents the first time they're used big. There's history of more capable groups pushing out less capable ones. Weak comparisons, it says. But *"weak" is not "zero."*

And then the thing about the tournament, where nobody moved. It gave that real weight. But it said it proves something smaller than the critics think: *Two groups that stay at 1% and 6% after months of debate have both rejected 50% and both rejected 0.001%. That is information.* The fact that they couldn't agree tells you how open the question is. It doesn't tell you the question is empty.

It was not impressed that the survey's middle answer stayed at 5 percent for seven years. The skeptic in me wanted that to be meaningful and it said no: *Five percent is a round, comfortable number*. It said a number that doesn't move could be a belief that doesn't move, or just a comfortable place to put your finger.

And then it did two things I didn't expect.

It went after LeCun's point about agency, the kitchen fire thing, and said we put odds on things we control all the time: *odds a company fails, odds a marriage lasts, odds a patient recovers if treated*. The number just assumes people behave the way people behave.

And it went after Yudkowsky. The man who says the numbers are astrology, and whose book title is *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*. And it said, and this is exactly right and I'm annoyed I didn't say it: *That is a number in prose.* You can't refuse to give odds and then put "everyone dies" on the cover. And it said LeCun's *essentially zero* is the same move from the other direction. Both of them are giving you a number while saying numbers are silly.

Most wrong on this one: Yampolskiy. It called his 99.9 percent *a position stated as a measurement*. No end date, no breakdown, no way to ever be wrong.

Most right: Ord, for saying that refusing to estimate is itself an estimate.

### Should you trust the superforecasters over the experts?

**Verdict: Overstated. 35 percent.**

That's 35 percent that the superforecasters' 0.4 to 1 percent is closer to the truth than the experts' 3 to 6 percent.[^13]

And it really gave the forecasters their due first. It said the foxes-beat-hedgehogs finding is *one of the more robust results in the study of prediction*. It said the selection problem is real: the people who became AI-risk experts are *a census of people who were worried before they looked*. That's a great line. And it said the forecasters' low number is a useful reminder that most predicted catastrophes don't happen.

But then here's why it went against them. The forecasters got good at questions that resolve in a few months or a few years, that have a track record to lean on, where the winning move is start at the usual rate and adjust slowly. And this question breaks every one of those. No usual rate, decades out, and the event is exactly the kind of sudden break that a start-from-the-usual method *underweights by construction*. It said the method isn't wrong. It's just being used outside the range where it was ever shown to work.

Then it added something from its own general knowledge, not from anything in our sources, so take it that way: it said, with moderate confidence, that the same tournament also asked about nearer-term AI milestones, and the superforecasters came in too low on several of them compared to what actually happened. If that's right, the generalists were underestimating exactly the part of the question where knowing the field helps most.

And timing. The tournament was 2022. Before the current machines, before the lab tests, before the labs said their AI writes most of their code.[^36] Nobody re-asked the forecasters with any of that in hand.

It also caught me doing something sloppy, or caught the scoreboard doing it, which is the same thing. It said you can't line up Amodei's number, Hinton's, and Kokotajlo's on the same ladder as the forecasters' extinction number, because they're answering different questions. Amodei's covers *catastrophically wrong on the scale of human civilization*, including people misusing AI. Hinton's is *just gut*. Kokotajlo's is *goes horribly wrong*, which is bigger than extinction. Apples and apocalypses.

Its blended answer for extinction alone by 2100: roughly 3 to 4 percent. Inside the experts' range. Several times the forecasters'.

Most right: Narayanan and Kapoor, for the selection effect and the hedgehogs. Most wrong: LeCun again, because he said *most leading AI figures* think the risk is essentially zero, and a survey of 2,778 of those figures came back with a middle answer of 5 percent.[^14]

### Is AI the biggest risk to humanity this century?

**Verdict: Mostly sound. 70 percent for the ranking. 45 percent that Ord's 1 in 10 is right within a factor of two.**

So it believes the order more than it believes the number. And the reason is the best thing in the whole ruling.

It says AI is *the one risk on the list with an agent that could finish the job*.

A pandemic leaves survivors, and they rebuild. A nuclear war leaves survivors, and they rebuild. Climate change leaves survivors. None of those things *wants* anything. But an AI that's taken control doesn't have to kill everyone to make it permanent. It just has to keep control. That's why it goes to the top of the list even if you think Ord's exact number is too high. It's the only thing on the list that could want us to stay down.

It took Narayanan and Kapoor's argument seriously here too, the one where humans with AI help will keep up with AI. It said that works if AI improves gradually, if lots of systems keep each other in check, and if the AI helping the humans is actually on the humans' side. But then: *The tool-user is only powerful while the tool obeys.* If the helper AI is the same kind of thing you're worried about, then humans-plus-AI versus AI isn't two teams. And this is the line: *they are one competitor and its hostage.*

On Bender and Hanna's argument that doom talk distracts from real present harms, it said that's *a serious argument about politics. It is not an argument about magnitude.* Whether something is inconvenient to talk about doesn't tell you how big it is.

Most right: Ord, for seeing that the special thing about AI risk is *permanently ceded control*, not body count. Most wrong: Andrew Ng and his Mars. It pointed out that within ten years of that line, the field's typical researcher was at 5 percent.[^14]

### The opening number

Now here's the number this whole book starts from. The chance that AI itself, not people using it as a weapon, causes human extinction or permanently takes the future out of human hands. Any route. Later chapters take the routes apart one at a time, and at the end of the book we come back and see if this holds.

|---|---|---|
| 2035 | 6% | 2.5% |
| 2050 | 9% | 6% |
| 2100 | 10% | 8% |

About 8 percent by 2100. Roughly one in twelve.

And I'm going to leave that number exactly where it is, because it is what the evaluator ruled at the start, and because it does not survive the book. In Chapter 12 it raises this figure to **10 percent**, against its own earlier work, after finding it had underrated a mechanism it had treated as an afterthought. In Chapter 16 it audits the whole ledger and takes 2050 back down. The final bill is in Chapter 16. What you are looking at here is the opening bid, and the fact that you can watch it move is the entire reason this book pays somebody else to do the arithmetic.

And it defined "superhuman" on purpose, higher than most people mean by "AGI": AI that *clearly exceeds the best humans at nearly all cognitive work, including AI research itself*, and can work on its own on long tasks.

Here's how it built the 8 percent. Four pieces, each one something you can argue with:

- **85 percent** that superhuman AI gets built by 2100 at all. The trends are fast and there are 75 years of runway. The other 15 percent is a plateau, a deliberate stop, or the world falling apart first.
- **50 percent** that at least one system that matters ends up with goals seriously at odds with human control. It called that *a coin flip* and said *my honest read*.
- **35 percent** that the misaligned system actually gets a decisive, lasting advantage, fast or slow, instead of being checked by other systems that stayed on our side.
- **55 percent** that if it gets there, it's permanent, not something humanity recovers from.

Multiply: 0.85 × 0.50 × 0.35 × 0.55 comes to about 0.082. I checked. Eight percent.

And it explained the shape over time. Most of the risk sits in the transition, the first decades after the thing arrives. That's why the number jumps from 2.5 percent in 2035 to 6 percent in 2050 and then only creeps to 8 by 2100. If we get through the first few decades, we'll have learned a lot, and the risk falls.

Now line it up against the humans.[^14][^13][^5][^20][^21][^26][^27][^11]

It's between the AI researchers' two middle answers, 5 percent for extinction or disempowerment and 10 percent for loss of control, and below both averages. It's far above the superforecasters, and it says partly that's because it counts permanent disempowerment and not just extinction; its extinction-only number, 3 to 4 percent, is still several times theirs. It's inside the experts' range on extinction. It's just under Ord's 1 in 10, and it says the reason is funny: Ord was too low on AI arriving this century and a little too high on how bad it goes once it arrives, and those two mistakes almost cancel. It's well below Christiano. Far below Kokotajlo, Tegmark, Yampolskiy and Yudkowsky. Far above LeCun.

And about the polls, the 63 percent of Americans who said AI *could someday destroy mankind*,[^33] it said that's a different question. *Could* is about possibility, and *most careful estimators would also say it could.*

And about prediction markets on human extinction, from its own general knowledge, it made a point so obvious I'm embarrassed nobody at dinner made it: *nobody can collect on a "yes."* You can't win that bet. There's no one left to pay you.

### The bottom line

Its answer to the chapter's question, what do you do with a spread from zero to 99.9:

It says the spread is mostly people answering different questions. Some are counting extinction by 2100. Some are counting "things go badly," no date. Some include humans using AI as a weapon and some don't. Line them all up on the same question, and *the serious ones cluster between about 1 percent and 20 percent, with a center in the high single digits.*

And the two ends? *99.9 percent has no horizon and no way to be wrong, and "essentially zero" is a number pulled from the same thin air its author accuses everyone else of using.*

Which numbers should you take seriously? The ones that come with a date, a definition of what they're counting, and pieces you can argue with one at a time.

And then this, which is where I'll leave you: *the risk is not zero, it is not certain, and it is large enough that 1 percent and 10 percent call for the same response.*

## Key Takeaways

The dinner question was "what are the odds?" Here's what you actually learned.

- **The humans who study this have a number, and it's all over the place.** *p(doom)*, the probability of doom, runs from Yann LeCun's "essentially zero" to Roman Yampolskiy's 99.9 percent. The typical AI researcher says 5 percent. Superforecasters say around 0.4. Lab leaders and Turing Award winners cluster around 10 to 25. The US public says 63 percent think AI *could* destroy us someday.
- **Several of the most important humans refuse to give a number.** Sam Altman and Demis Hassabis decline. Eliezer Yudkowsky, the author with the scariest book title on the shelf, calls these numbers astrology, and the ">95 percent" floating around online under his name isn't well sourced.
- **Months of structured debate moved nobody.** Superforecasters and AI experts argued the question for months and ended with *minimal convergence*. The evaluator read this as a measure of how open the question is, not proof that it's empty.
- **Do the numbers mean anything?** The evaluator ruled *Mostly sound*, at **80 percent**: they carry real information at the level of "one in a thousand versus one in ten," never at the level of decimal points. Refusing to estimate is itself an estimate, and a book title that says everyone dies is *a number in prose*.
- **Should you trust the superforecasters over the experts?** *Overstated*, at **35 percent**. The forecasters' method was proven on short, repeatable questions, and this question is neither. The evaluator's blended estimate for AI-caused extinction alone by 2100 is roughly **3 to 4 percent**, inside the experts' range.
- **Is AI the biggest risk to humanity this century?** *Mostly sound*: **70 percent** that it outranks engineered pandemics, nuclear war and climate change, but only **45 percent** that Toby Ord's specific 1 in 10 is right within a factor of two. The reason AI ranks first: it's the only risk on the list with an agent that could want humanity to stay down.
- **The opening number, later revised.** The evaluator's opening estimate that AI itself causes human extinction or permanent disempowerment: **2.5 percent by 2035, 6 percent by 2050, 8 percent by 2100** overall, and **6, 9 and 10 percent** if superhuman AI has been built by each date. The breakdown: 85 percent superhuman AI gets built × 50 percent it ends up with goals at odds with human control × 35 percent it gains a decisive, lasting advantage × 55 percent the result is permanent. *It's not a prophecy.* It's a list of four premises you're invited to argue with. And it does not hold: the evaluator raises the 2100 figure to **10 percent** in Chapter 12 and re-audits the whole ledger in Chapter 16.
- **Where that sits.** Near the AI researcher survey, just under Ord, inside the experts' extinction range, well above the superforecasters, far below Kokotajlo, Tegmark, Yampolskiy and Yudkowsky, and far above LeCun.
- **The part that matters at dinner.** The serious numbers cluster between 1 and 20 percent, and 1 percent and 10 percent call for the same response.

Nobody at the table gets to say zero.
