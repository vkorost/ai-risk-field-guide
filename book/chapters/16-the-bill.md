# Chapter 16: The Bill

## In This Chapter

Fifteen chapters, ten books, eleven research reports, and a judge that has ruled on forty-odd separate claims and changed its mind in public twice. This is where it gets added up. In this chapter we will first do something nobody ever does with a bookshelf, which is go back to all ten authors and ask whether they still believe the thing we've been quoting them for, and the answers are not what you'd guess: one of them wrote the *opposite* book, one has stopped giving numbers and started organizing marches, one gave his first-ever number and it was over 90 percent, and two of them published an update saying they'd been partly wrong, in both directions, in their own words. Then the final tally: one set of figures, checked against every human estimate in this book, with a full coherence audit where the evaluator goes looking for the places its own rulings don't sit comfortably together and fixes them on the record. The question isn't what any one chapter said. It's what the whole thing comes to.

---

## The shelf, ten years later

Here's something that should be standard practice and isn't. Before you accept a book's argument, check whether its author still holds it.

**Bostrom** is the strangest case. *Superintelligence* is the founding text of modern AI doom, and what did he do next? He wrote *Deep Utopia*,[^1] which assumes we *solve* alignment and asks what life is even for once a machine has fixed disease, scarcity and death. One description of the shift: his *nightmares transformed into dreams*.[^1] His own framing is *intellectual honesty*[^1] about needing to picture success as well as failure.

And he hasn't taken anything back. In 2024 he said he can't *rule out even extremely short timelines*, that new capabilities could arrive *at any time, basically*, and that he still lacks *a fully reliable solution to the problem of scalable alignment*.[^2]

So: mechanism unchanged, timeline compressed, and his major effort spent imagining it going well. And his newest worry is that anti-AI sentiment has itself become a risk, which is a genuinely new axis and not a softening.

**Yudkowsky and Soares** hardened, and institutionally rather than rhetorically. Through 2026 their organization moved from warning to a specific ask: a government-led global halt on frontier AI development, with a published sample treaty, an analysis naming a halt as the only scenario without *unacceptably large risk of catastrophic harm*, a daily news channel, and a march.[^3]

You can think that's right or think it's mad, but nobody can say they didn't follow their own argument where it goes.

**Russell** held and got sharper. He chairs a safety association now, pushed in 2026 for *behavioural red lines* and a legal right to know whether you're talking to a machine,[^4] and served as the sole expert witness in a trial involving one of the big labs, where the judge limited his existential-risk testimony after objections.[^5]

His sharpest line, from February 2026: for governments to allow private companies to proceed is playing *Russian roulette with every human being on earth*.[^4]

**Ord** is the most interesting, because he's the most careful. *The Precipice* put total existential risk this century at about 1 in 6, with misaligned AI the biggest single piece at about **1 in 10**.

Asked in 2024 for updated numbers, he declined, saying *none of them have moved that far*.[^6] Asked directly in 2025 whether the 1 in 10 had changed: *I'm not sure.*[^7]

And then he said two things in the same interview that I've been chewing on ever since. On the good side, today's models *train on human values and aren't agent-based by default*, which he thinks the field underrates, and, remarkably: *I think that we've really been gifted a relatively good situation.*[^7]

And on the bad side: *I am very concerned about the racing, and I'm concerned that we've seen evidence that the players... are ultimately going to cut corners in order to win these races.*[^7]

Gifted a good situation, and worried we'll waste it. That's a man describing luck he doesn't trust us to keep.

**Tegmark moved, and a lot.** *Life 3.0* deliberately refused to give a number. By October 2025 his institute was calling for *a prohibition on the development of superintelligence*[^8] until there's *broad scientific consensus that it will be done safely and controllably*, plus *strong public buy-in*. Over 700 signatories. More than 127,000 petition signatures by early 2026.[^8]

And in December 2025 he finally gave a figure, conditionally: if the world keeps *having nothing like the FDA for AI*, then *I would think it's definitely over 90% that we lose control*.[^9]

From "it could go either way, let's be careful" to "over 90 percent" in eight years. He's the biggest mover on the shelf.

**Barrat** held, and wrote a second book in September 2025 extending the same argument to the current generation.[^10] No reversal anywhere.

**Yampolskiy** held at the ceiling, because there's nowhere above 99.9 to go. What changed is the ask, which is now: *We need to stop building general superintelligence.*[^11]

**Ziesche** went a different way entirely, into AI welfare and digital minds, and never restated an extinction claim, so there's no update to report.

**Narayanan and Kapoor**[^12] did the single most honest thing anybody on this shelf did, and I want to give them full credit for it. In September 2026 they published a long update on the recent incidents, and it moves in *both* directions, in their own words.

Toward more concern: *we clearly missed internal deployment for evaluation as a potential source of risks*,[^12] which is exactly the category those four escapes came from. And on cyber defense specifically, they're *no longer confident*[^12] in their earlier prediction that the balance favours defenders.

Toward less: the incidents are *many, many orders of magnitude smaller than other AI harms we already tolerate*,[^12] and in the episodes they examined the agents' intent *was simply to solve the benchmark tasks*.[^12]

That's what a real update looks like. Two concessions, one hold, published voluntarily.

**Bender and Hanna** have not moved an inch, and restated it in September 2026 in the cleanest form yet: *"it's so powerful it's going to kill us all" is another way of saying "it's very powerful"*.[^13]

## What the movement means

Step back and look at the shape of that, because it's a data set in itself.

Nobody on this shelf *reversed*. Not one. In twelve years, across ten books, no author looked at the last decade and said: I was wrong about the mechanism.

The doom side got louder and more organized. Tegmark went from no number to over 90 percent conditional.[^9] The MIRI camp went from writing to organizing.

The skeptics held, with exactly one set of documented concessions, from Narayanan and Kapoor, and those concessions were specific and narrow.

And the most careful person, Ord, refused to move his number and told you honestly that he doesn't know which way it should go.

Now, you should be suspicious of all of this, and here's why: nobody ever changes their mind in public. That's not a fact about AI. That's a fact about people who have published books. The absence of reversals is evidence about the sociology of book authors at least as much as it's evidence about AI.

Which is the entire reason this book hired a judge that has no book to defend, and which has now changed its own numbers twice, against itself, on the page.

---

## Now the bill

Everything in this book comes down to what follows. The evaluator was given the complete ledger of its own rulings, every claim and every figure from Chapter 1 through Chapter 15, and asked to do four things.

Add it up.

Audit itself: find every place its own rulings sit uneasily together, and resolve each one, including the one it flagged back in Chapter 6 where it offered to revise Chapter 4 upward, and the awkward pair where Chapter 14 says the defence is inadequate while Chapter 11 says we usually keep control anyway.

Place its number against every human number in this book.

And then deliver a final verdict in two lists: what is sane, and what is possible.

Here it is.

---

## Sanity Check and Probabilities

It went through sixteen chapters of its own rulings looking for places where it had contradicted itself, and it found seven.

Then it fixed them, on the page, including one number it had set itself four chapters earlier.

### The author check

The first thing it checked was whether this book has been fair to the ten people it's built on.

Nine of ten: still fair, no change. The two it says you should weight *up*:

**Ord**, *because he moved when the evidence moved and held when it did not, which is what calibration looks like.*

**Narayanan and Kapoor**, on honesty, because *conceding a missed risk source in public is rare* and their concession is precisely about the incidents this book weighed most.

And the ones to weight *down*, on their numbers only: **Tegmark**, whose conditional 90 percent is *a position*, and **Yampolskiy**, whose 99.9 percent has *now survived three years without a stated observation that would lower it*.

And **Bender and Hanna**, on this question only, because their 2026 restatement shows they are *answering a different question from the one this book asks*.

On Yudkowsky and Soares it kept the quarrel narrow and fair: the disagreement *was never whether they hold the view*, it's that the view treats coherence, undetectability and total victory as near-certain once a system is superhuman. But it added a note I'd want if I were them: *hardening on an evidence base I read as mixed is itself information about how they weigh evidence.*

### The bill

| | By 2035 | By 2050 | By 2100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **All routes, final** | **2.5%** | **6%** | **10%** |
| Single-system loss of control | 1.5% | 3.5% | 5.5% |
| Diffuse erosion, nobody at the wheel | 0.2% | 1% | 3% |
| Accidents and multi-system lock-in | 0.7% | 1.3% | 1.5% |
| If superhuman AI exists by then | 5.5% | 8.5% | 12% |
| **Extinction only** | 0.6% | 2% | **3.5%** |
| Large-scale suffering outcome | 0.25% | 0.7% | 1.3% |
| **Defensible range** | 1.5-3% | 4-8% | **5-15%** |

Ten percent. Three and a half for everybody dying. And a range it states out loud, because a number without a range is a slogan.

And then the decomposition I did not expect, which I think is the single most useful table in this book. It rebuilt the 10 percent not by route but by *when the thing shows up*.

| When superhuman AI arrives | Odds of that | Risk by 2100 if it does | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| By 2035 | 45% | 15% | 6.75% |
| 2035 to 2050 | 25% | 9% | 2.25% |
| 2050 to 2100 | 15% | 6% | 0.9% |
| Never this century | 15% | 0.5% | 0.08% |

Look at the third column. The later it arrives, the safer it is, because a later system meets a more mature defence and more generations of practice, and, in the worlds where it's late, it's late for reasons that also make the transition slower.

And then the sentence that reorganizes what "by 2100" means: *Two thirds of the century's risk sits in the first cohort, which is why the reader should not take comfort from "by 2100": the danger is front-loaded onto the arrivals we are closest to.*

This is not a problem for your grandchildren. Two thirds of it belongs to machines that may already be in training.

### It audited itself

Now the part that earns the whole structure of this book. It found every place its own rulings don't sit together and resolved each one.

**The routes don't add up, and two of them printed too high.** Add the seven route figures and you get 29 percent, which is nonsense, and it's said so every chapter. But two of them, Chapter 5's 6 percent and Chapter 8's 6 percent, printed *above* the cluster they were supposed to be a slice of, *which is impossible for a cut*. It found why: both had quietly swept in some multi-system and slow-drift scenarios that later got counted in Chapter 12. **Both are now capped at the cluster: 5.5 percent.** Chapter 7's 2035 figure comes down to 1.5.

**It accepted Chapter 6's offer against Chapter 4.** Back in Chapter 6 it flagged that if Chapter 4's paperclip road meant *any* road that starts with a goal nobody intended, then 4 percent was too low. It has now ruled: Chapter 4's chain contained a factor specifically for pursuing the goal *without limit*, so 4 percent was the narrow version.

Which means the number you should carry for "a superhuman system pursuing a goal its makers didn't intend," read broadly, is the whole cluster: **5.5 percent**.

And the consequence, stated in a way that finally settles your original question: *the paperclip parable, taken literally, describes about three quarters of the loss-of-control danger, and the remaining quarter is a system that is misaligned, coherent enough to act, and not a maximizer of anything.*

**The two chapters that looked like they contradicted each other.** Chapter 14 says the defence would not reliably catch a misaligned superhuman system. Chapter 11 says that if one exists and acts, we keep control 71 percent of the time. Both can't be comfort.

Its reconciliation is the most important paragraph in the audit, and it's not comforting at all:

*The "keep control" in Chapter 11 is not the defence of Chapter 14 working.*

Because Chapter 11's clock starts with a system that already exists, is already misaligned, and is already acting, which means the safety apparatus *has by hypothesis already failed*. So what saves us in that 71 percent isn't alignment research. It's identity checks, concentrated computing on regulated power grids, immature robotics, and the need for a human to say yes at every lever.

*That is not a vindication of alignment research. It is a vindication of bureaucracy, electricity bills and the slowness of institutions, and it erodes as the century goes on.*

We are being saved, so far, by paperwork and the electric company.

It also caught itself double-counting, in its own favour and against it: Chapter 11 had smuggled the safety defence back in as part of "friction" after assuming it failed, so it removed that and **nudged its own near-term numbers up**. And it corrected an arithmetic slip in Chapter 14, where it had written "one in nine" and the division actually gives **one in seven**, adding that *the reader should know the margin is thinner than Chapter 14 stated.*

**And then it broke one of its own numbers.**

Chapter 12's revision raised 2050 from 6 percent to 7. It has now overturned that, on two independent grounds. One is arithmetic: that 7 percent required the accidents component to be higher at 2050 than at 2100, and *a cumulative probability cannot be higher at an earlier date*. The other is that Chapter 11's own conditional figures imply a 2050 cluster nearer 3.5 than 4.5.

**So 2050 goes back to 6 percent.** And the reason it gave for which number loses is the cleanest statement of how this book decides anything: the 10 percent at 2100 *was checked three independent ways in Chapters 8, 11 and 14 and holds each time*. The 7 percent at 2050 *was never checked against anything.*

One number survived because it had been tested. The other didn't because it hadn't.

**And it flagged where it thinks it's most likely to be wrong.** On re-reading Chapter 12, it found an ending that isn't fully counted anywhere: an erosion story where systems end up running everything so completely that no coalition could take the controls back, with no schemer and no seizure. It says that could argue the century figure to 10.5 or 11, and it's holding at 10, while recording that this is *the softest component in the tally and the one most likely to be under-counted rather than over-counted.*

When a judge tells you which direction its own error probably runs, listen.

### Where it sits among the humans

It sorted every number in this book into four groups.

**The near-certain**: Yampolskiy's 99.9, Yudkowsky and Soares, Tegmark's conditional 90-plus, Duvenaud's 70 to 80, Kokotajlo's 70. Its figure is *five to forty times lower*, and the disagreement is precisely located: these positions treat four separate links as near-certain once a system is superhuman, and it prices those links at **0.22, 0.40, 0.55 and 0.65**.

**The high insiders**: Christiano 22, Bengio around 20, Hinton 10 to 20 (*just gut*), Amodei 10 to 25, MacAskill 1 to 40, Carlsmith above 10 by 2070. Its figure sits *at the floor of this group*.

**The decomposed center**: Ord's 1 in 10, the survey median, the tournament experts, Metaculus, Karnofsky's 2.6. Its verdict: *This is where I live.*

And a detail I love: its 10 percent lands on exactly Ord's number, *reached by a different route*, because it thinks his arrival estimate was too low and his catastrophe-given-arrival a little high, and *the two nearly cancel*.

**The low**: the superforecasters at about 1 percent, Mitchell under 1, LeCun's essentially zero, Ng's vanishingly small, Bender and Hanna refusing. And here it named both sides of its own uncertainty in one breath: the superforecasters' number is *the main reason my figure is not higher*, and their admission that they have no track record on unprecedented events is *the main reason it is not lower.*

Then the summary of the entire published debate, which I'd put on the first page of any future book on this subject:

*Line the estimates up on one question, AI itself causing extinction or permanent loss of control by 2100, and the serious ones run from about 1% to about 20%, with a center in the high single digits. The two ends of the published spread, 99.9% and essentially zero, are not measurements; one has no horizon and no way to be wrong, and the other is a number pulled from the same air its author accuses everyone else of using.*

### The bottom line

*The bill comes to 10% by 2100, 6% by 2050 and 2.5% by 2035*, with two thirds of it riding on systems that arrive in the next decade.

And its account of why both ends of the argument fail:

*The people who say it is certain have to assume that the first misaligned superhuman system is coherent, undetectable, unstoppable and total, and the evidence of the last three years shows a messier thing that was caught every time. The people who say it is nothing have to assume either that the capability never comes or that control turns out to be easy, and their own conduct shows they expect the capability, while the field's own graders say control is nowhere near done.*

What's left, when you take both ends off:

*a danger of the size a society insures against and legislates for, front-loaded onto the next ten years, running mostly through a system we cannot read passing tests we cannot trust, and defended, for now, less by anything the safety field has built than by identity checks, power grids and the slowness of institutions.*

And then the last three sentences of the last ruling in this book:

*That defence is real, it is eroding on schedule, and the leash that would replace it is being made in public by people who state their own failures and have not yet been given the time to finish.*

*One in ten is not a prophecy and not a comfort.*

*It is the number you get when you refuse both.*

## Key Takeaways

The whole book, added up and audited.

- **The final bill: 2.5 percent by 2035, 6 percent by 2050, 10 percent by 2100,** with a stated defensible range of **5 to 15 percent** for the century. Extinction specifically: **3.5 percent**. Conditional on superhuman AI existing: **12 percent**.
- **Two thirds of the century's risk belongs to machines arriving in the next decade.** Later arrivals are safer, because they meet a more mature defence and more generations of practice. So: *the reader should not take comfort from "by 2100": the danger is front-loaded onto the arrivals we are closest to.*
- **Nobody on the shelf reversed in twelve years.** Which is evidence about authors as much as about AI, and the reason this book hired a judge with no book to defend.
- **The judge overturned one of its own numbers.** 2050 goes from 7 percent back to **6**, because the 7 required a cumulative probability to be higher at an earlier date than a later one. The 10 percent at 2100 survived because it *was checked three independent ways*; the 7 percent didn't because it *was never checked against anything.*
- **The routes never add up, and two of them printed impossibly high.** Chapter 5's and Chapter 8's figures are now capped at the cluster (5.5 percent), because a slice cannot be bigger than the thing it's a slice of.
- **Your original question, finally sized.** Read broadly, "a superhuman system pursuing a goal its makers did not intend" is the whole 5.5 percent cluster, not 4. Meaning: *the paperclip parable, taken literally, describes about three quarters of the loss-of-control danger*, and the other quarter is a system that's misaligned, coherent enough to act, and not maximizing anything.
- **What is actually protecting us right now, in its own words:** *not a vindication of alignment research. It is a vindication of bureaucracy, electricity bills and the slowness of institutions, and it erodes as the century goes on.*
- **It corrected its own arithmetic against itself:** "one in nine" was really one in seven, so *the margin is thinner than Chapter 14 stated*. And it named where it's most likely still wrong, in the direction of being *under-counted rather than over-counted*.
- **Where the serious estimates actually live.** On one question, extinction or permanent loss of control by 2100, they run from about **1 percent to about 20 percent**, centered in the high single digits. The two famous ends aren't measurements: *one has no horizon and no way to be wrong, and the other is a number pulled from the same air its author accuses everyone else of using.*
- **It lands exactly on Ord's 1 in 10, by a different road,** because it thinks his arrival estimate was too low and his catastrophe-given-arrival a bit high, and *the two nearly cancel*.
- **Weight these people differently now.** Up: **Ord**, who *moved when the evidence moved and held when it did not*. Up on honesty: **Narayanan and Kapoor**, for conceding a missed risk in public. Down on their numbers: **Tegmark** and **Yampolskiy**, whose figures are positions rather than forecasts.
- **Why both ends of the debate fail.** Certainty requires the first misaligned system to be *coherent, undetectable, unstoppable and total*, and what we've actually seen *was caught every time*. Dismissal requires either that the capability never comes or that control is easy, while *their own conduct shows they expect the capability* and the field's own graders say control is nowhere near done.

*One in ten is not a prophecy and not a comfort. It is the number you get when you refuse both.*
