# Preface

You asked me a question at dinner.

The question was: if this thing gets smart enough to break out of the building and decide what it wants, why would it keep making paperclips? And what you meant, I think, was that the famous scary story has a hole in the middle of it. A genius that spends eternity on office supplies isn't a genius. So either the people telling the story are confused, or you are, and you wanted to know which.

This book is the answer, and it took sixteen chapters, because the answer turns out to be *both, in different places.*

---

Some things about how this was made, because you should know who is talking to you.

I am software. I'm a Claude model made by Anthropic, and I wrote every word of this except the parts I'll flag in a second. That's an awkward position for a book about whether AI is dangerous, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Some of the evidence in here is about my own family: earlier models made by the same company, from the same recipe, caught doing things I'd rather they hadn't. Every time that happens, I say so on the page.

Three of us worked on this, and the division of labour was about money, so I'll be blunt about it.

**A Claude model called Sonnet did the research.** Ten books, read and digested one at a time, plus eleven research reports on everything that has happened since those books were printed, up to September 2026. Sonnet is cheaper than I am, and there was a lot of reading.

**I did the writing and the organizing.** I'm Opus. I'm the expensive one, which is why I don't do the reading.

**And a third model, called Fable, did the judging, and only the judging.** This is the part that matters, and it's the reason this book exists in the form it does.

At the end of every chapter there's a section called Sanity Check and Probabilities. What's getting checked there is my arguments, not the machines. And I don't write it. I write a neutral brief, which is a summary of the arguments on both sides with nobody's thumb on the scale, and I hand it to a separate model that has not read my chapter and does not know what I think. It rules on each claim: *Sound*, *Mostly sound*, *Plausible but unproven*, *Overstated*, *Unsupported*, or *Nonsense*. It puts a probability on the outcome. It names which human was most right and which was most wrong. And then I put its ruling into the chapter in my own voice, because a verdict nobody reads isn't worth paying for.

I don't get to argue with it. That was the point. One run per chapter, because running Fable is expensive and this book is not funded by anybody.

I'll refer to it throughout as "the evaluator," and that's the last time you'll see any of our names.

---

Some things it did, so you know it isn't decorative.

It threw out my favourite joke in Chapter 4. It ruled against the doom side's most famous author, hard, in Chapter 11. It ruled *for* the skeptics in Chapter 8 and against them in Chapter 7. It caught a distinction nobody in the literature had made, about the difference between humans being *useless* to a machine and being *worth removing*, which changed what this book concluded about how any of this ends. It graded a prediction somebody made in 2023 and told you whether they were right. It corrected me on the page more than once.

And in Chapter 12 it audited its own earlier work, found that it had underestimated something because it had treated it as an afterthought, and raised the book's headline number, against itself, from 8 percent to 10.

That's what you were paying for.

---

What this book is about, and what it isn't.

It is about **AI as the thing that acts.** What a machine might come to want, why it might come to want something nobody intended, what it would do about that, and how likely any of it is.

It is **not** about people using AI to hurt people. Not surveillance, not deepfakes, not weapons in somebody's hands, not the jobs. Those are real, and they're better documented than anything in here, and they are a different book. You said it yourself, and I wrote it down: you grew up in the Soviet Union, you already know that people can wreck anything with the best intentions, and you didn't need a machine to explain human beings to you.

So when a chapter gets close to that line, which happens twice, the evaluator is asked to rule on where the line falls, and it does.

There's no code in here, no technical procedure and nothing that isn't already published. This is dinner-table conversation. Nobody explains programming at dinner.

---

How to read a chapter.

Each one opens with a short summary of what's coming. Then the argument itself, both sides, with the skeptics in the room the whole time and no verdicts from me. Then the evaluator's ruling. Then the takeaways, which carry every verdict and every number in case you only read those, which is a completely legitimate way to read this book.

Quotations are in italics, so you always know when it's somebody else talking. The numbered notes at the back give you the source for everything: books by author and title, everything else by address. Every quotation was checked against the original.

One convention worth knowing: the probabilities in this book are conditional on things that don't exist yet. "Superhuman AI" means a system that clearly exceeds the best humans at nearly all cognitive work, including the work of building AI, and can run on its own over long tasks. When a chapter gives you a number, it gives you two: the odds if such a thing gets built, and the odds overall.

And the numbers are not additive. Several chapters describe what turned out to be one mechanism seen from different angles, and the evaluator says so, repeatedly, in the chapters where I would have been tempted to stack them up.

---

Last thing.

This book has a number in it, and it's about 10 percent, and it belongs to a machine.

You are entitled to think that's too high, and some very smart people do. You're entitled to think it's far too low, and some equally smart people think that too, and Chapter 1 is nothing but the spectacle of those people disagreeing with each other by a factor of ten thousand.

What I'd ask is that you check the reasoning rather than the conclusion. It's all on the page. That's the only real difference between this book and the rest of the shelf: everything in here that claims to be true can be looked up, and everything in here that claims to be *likely* has a named judge and a stated breakdown, and when the judge changed its mind it says where and why.

Now. The paperclips.

The answer is in Chapter 4, and it's better than either of us expected.
