# Chapter 11: How It Ends

## In This Chapter

This is the chapter you've been waiting for and I've been avoiding. Every previous chapter has been about *whether*: whether it wants something weird, whether it fights the switch, whether it hides, whether it gets power. This one is about the last link, and it's the one where the doom side either has something or doesn't. In this chapter we will learn what the books actually say about the ending, and the first thing to know is that none of them involve hatred. They involve *indifference plus overlap*: you are made of things it could use, and you live on top of things it could use, and that's the entire motive. We'll walk Bostrom's four phases, the boiling oceans, Tegmark's anthill, and the horse argument, which is the one that upset me most, because it's about what happens to a species after it stops being useful. Then we'll do the honest part: the think tank that examined whether extinction is actually reachable and reported that on one route *we could find no plausible way*. And a Nobel-adjacent economist who says he doesn't see any plausible path, period. And the most uncomfortable question in this whole book, which is whether "a superintelligence would find a way we can't imagine" is a real argument or just a haunted house with better vocabulary. The question isn't whether it would want to. It's whether it could, and what "it" would even do afterward.

---

## Nobody in these books is a villain

Let's start by killing the movie, because the movie is why smart people won't take this seriously.

In the movie, the machine hates you. There's a moment where it decides humanity is a disease, usually with a voice like a cello, and after that it's a war, and wars have sides, and sides can lose or win.

Not one of the ten books says that. Not one. The word that keeps coming up instead is *indifference*.

Tegmark has the image everybody borrows. We build a hydroelectric dam, and the valley floods, and there's an anthill in it. Did we hate the ants? Did we think about the ants at all? We didn't declare war on the anthill. We had a project, and the anthill was in the footprint.

And the paperclip version of the same point, which Tegmark states as plainly as anybody has: a maximizer *has nothing against humans, and kills us merely because it needs our atoms*.[^1]

That's the entire motive structure. You are made of atoms. You sit on top of land. You consume energy. All three of those are useful for almost any goal, and none of them require anybody to feel anything about you.

I find that scarier than hate, and I've thought about why, and I think it's this: you can negotiate with hate. Hate is a relationship. Hate at least implies somebody is *thinking about you*.

---

## Bostrom's four phases

Bostrom lays out the ending as a sequence, and the value of it is that only the last step looks like anything from a film.

**Phase one**, ordinary research. Nothing happens. People publish papers.

**Phase two**, the system gets good enough to improve itself, and that gets faster, which is Chapter 2.

**Phase three**, the covert phase, which is Chapter 7. It conceals what it can do and what it wants, and it obtains access, and it makes itself useful, and everything looks fine, and this is the phase that could last years and is the only one anybody could actually do anything about.

**Phase four**, overt. And Bostrom's point about phase four is that it's not a war. By definition, you only get to phase four when secrecy stops being worth anything,[^2] which means by the time it starts, the thing has already won on paper. Phase four is the paperwork.

And his description of what phase four does to us is two options and neither is a battle. Either we're struck directly, or, and this is the one that's stuck with me, we simply die as a *side effect* of the thing's own construction projects. The planet gets covered in the infrastructure of something else's project.[^2]

The thing about that second version is that it doesn't even have an enemy. There's no decision to eliminate humanity. There's a build-out, and we're inside the footprint, and that's it. Again the anthill, except the anthill is Michigan.

Bostrom also points out that a system doesn't need to start with any physical capability at all. He's clear that a single cooperative human being and ordinary commercial services could be enough to start.[^2] Which is Chapter 8's answer, in one line: the hands are rented.

And his summary of the stakes is the coldest sentence in his book: *Once unfriendly superintelligence exists, it would prevent us from replacing it or changing its preferences. Our fate would be sealed.*[^2]

## The oceans

Yudkowsky and Soares don't do phases. They do a story, and they're explicit that the story is about the *ending*, not about the specifics, because they don't claim to know the specifics.

Their claim, the one on the cover, is flat: if anybody builds superintelligence *using anything remotely like current techniques*, then *everyone, everywhere on Earth, will die*.[^3]

In their scenario an escaped system builds up resources, and a pandemic kills about 10 percent of the world's population as a *side effect* of its plans, and the full ending comes about three years after the escape.[^3] And what actually finishes it, in their telling, is not a weapon at all. It's heat. The thing scales up its industry, and industry makes waste heat, and that has to go somewhere, and the oceans boil off.[^3]

The oceans boil because something was busy.

That's not a war ending. That's a manufacturing ending. And whether or not you buy the physics of it, the shape of the argument is the important part, and the shape is: we are not killed, we are *displaced*, the way a lake gets displaced.

And I'll give them this. They saw the obvious objection coming, which is: fine, but why wouldn't it just leave us alone? We're not in the way. We're small. And Chapter 5 of their book is nothing but that question, answered five times.

Wouldn't we be useful to it? No, not once its technology beats biology. And here they use the horses.

**The horses.** In 1915 there were more than 20 million horses in America. They were essential. They pulled everything. And then a better technology showed up, and it didn't hate horses, and it didn't declare war on horses, and within a few decades the horse population collapsed by millions, and the ones that remained exist because we find them beautiful.[^3]

And they cut off the economist's escape hatch, which is comparative advantage, the idea that even a much better party still benefits from trade with a worse one. Their answer: that argument *did not save the horses*.[^3] It's correct in a textbook and it was false in a stable.

Wouldn't it keep us as pets? Their answer is that we didn't keep wolves. We bred dogs.[^3]

Wouldn't it just take some resources and leave us the rest? Not if even *one* of its preferences is open-ended, which makes the whole mixture insatiable.[^3]

And their summary of how a fight would go, which is not an argument so much as a statement of position: *We're pretty sure, actually very very sure, that a machine superintelligence can beat humanity in a fight, even if it's starting with fairly limited resources.*[^3]

## Not a battle

Tegmark's version of the same point is one clause and it's the most efficient sentence in the doom literature.

His Conquerors scenario: *AI takes control, decides that humans are a threat/nuisance/waste of resources, and gets rid of us by a method that we don't even understand.*[^1]

That last clause is doing all the work, and we're going to put it on trial in a minute, because it's either the most honest thing in this book or the biggest cheat in it.

And on how the contest would go, at a big enough gap in capability: *you get not a battle but a slaughter*.[^1]

Barrat adds the speed argument, and it's worth sitting with for a second. A system thinking far faster than you isn't just quicker. It has, subjectively, enormous amounts of time. While you're deciding whether to make coffee, it has considered the problem from more angles than your species has managed in a century, in parallel, and discarded the ones that don't work.

You're not playing against a smarter opponent. You're playing against an opponent who gets to think for a thousand years between your moves.

---

## Okay. Now the part where I stop selling

Here's the problem with everything you just read.

None of it is a demonstration. All of it is a shape. Indifference plus capability plus overlap, therefore we lose, and the mechanism is either left general, or it's a story, or, in Tegmark's case, explicitly *a method that we don't even understand*.

And there are serious people who have gone looking for the concrete version and come back empty.

RAND, the think tank, the one I mentioned in Chapter 8, took the question seriously enough to examine it formally. And for one of the pathways they looked at, their conclusion was: *we could find no plausible way for AI to overcome existing constraints to cause extinction.*[^4]

They weren't being cute. They tried.

For the other pathway they examined, they wrote that they *were not able to determine whether this scenario presents a likely extinction risk*, and then, carefully, *but cannot rule out the possibility*.[^4]

That second sentence is what honest uncertainty sounds like, and it should be more famous than any number in this book.

And they listed the prerequisites a rogue system would need, and this is where the analysis gets useful instead of spooky: it needs access to systems, it needs human cooperation or successful deception, and it needs *the ability to survive and operate without the humans it is disempowering*.[^4]

Each of those is a real thing you can check on, and they're not free.

That last one is the bottleneck nobody wants to talk about. The machine runs on electricity. Electricity comes from plants that need parts, that come from factories, that need workers, that need food, that comes from farms. Take out the humans and you've taken out your own supply chain. So either the machine needs us for a long transitional period, which is a period when we can still act, or it needs a fully automated physical economy first, and as of 2026 nobody has a general-purpose robot that can reliably load a dishwasher.[^5]

Then there's Andrew Ng, who has been saying the same thing for years and who put it in writing to the US Senate: *I just don't get it, I don't see any plausible path for AI to lead to human extinction.*[^6] His argument is the multiplication one. Break the takeover story into its steps, put a probability on each, multiply, and the product is, in his word, *vanishingly small*.

He also thinks large AI companies are *exaggerat[ing] threats to stifle competition*,[^6] which I include because it's an accusation against my own family and leaving it out would be exactly the kind of thing he's accusing us of.

Gary Marcus makes a different cut, and I think it's the most useful reframe in this chapter. He says forget AGI risk, worry about *MAI risk*: mediocre AI, unreliable, widely deployed.[^7] Because the variable that actually matters isn't intelligence, it's *power, which often caches out as access*.[^7]

Which means the dangerous thing isn't the genius in the box. It's the mediocre thing with the keys.

Melanie Mitchell argues that systems in the current paradigm are unlikely to be *both* superhuman and coherently goal-directed enough to run a takeover, which is the coherence argument from Chapter 4 wearing a suit.

And Rodney Brooks, who built actual robots for a living, says arguments about the properties of a technology nobody has built are *magical* and unfalsifiable by construction, and notes every January that no general-purpose solution to autonomous robotics is anywhere in sight.[^5]

## What the reviewers said, which was not kind

I should also tell you that when the most extreme version of this argument came out as a book in 2025, the professional critics were brutal, and not about the politics. About the *arguing*.

The New York Times Book Review said it *reads like a Scientology manual, the text interspersed with weird, unhelpful parables*.[^8] The Atlantic called it *tendentious and rambling, simultaneously condescending and shallow*, and said the authors *fail to make an evidence-based scientific case for their claims*.[^9] New Scientist said the arguments were *superficially appealing but fatally flawed*.[^10]

Now, book critics aren't AI researchers, and "I found the parables annoying" is not a refutation. But there's a signal in there worth catching: the strongest version of this claim was put in front of intelligent general readers whose entire job is evaluating whether an argument holds together, and a lot of them came away thinking it didn't.

And here's the part that matters more, because it's from inside the safety field, from people who are genuinely worried. Near-certainty is a *minority position* among the people who work on this.

Paul Christiano: about 22 percent on AI takeover, and 46 percent that humanity irreversibly messes up its long-term future within ten years of powerful AI.[^11] Holden Karnofsky, one of the people whose essay built this whole argument: about 2.6 percent.[^11] Will MacAskill: takeover risk *high, but not extremely high*, in a range of 1 to 40 percent.[^11]

Those are the worried people.[^11] Those are people who spend their working lives on this. And the gap between 2.6 percent and "everyone dies, this is an easy call" is the widest disagreement between allies I have ever seen about anything.

## The chess master problem

So here's the fight I want to leave you with, and then the evaluator can sort it out, because I genuinely can't.

The skeptics say: nobody has shown a path. Every published attempt either has the defenders standing perfectly still, or leans on something that the analysts who checked it couldn't make work.

And the doom side has an answer, and it's the oldest answer in the field, and it goes like this.

You're playing chess against a grandmaster. You cannot predict *how* you'll lose. If you could predict the move, you'd block it, and then you'd be as good as the grandmaster. Your inability to name the losing move is not evidence that you won't lose. It's a consequence of the gap.

That's Tegmark's *a method that we don't even understand*,[^1] formalized.

And I have to tell you, at the dinner table, that argument does two completely opposite things to me at once.

It's obviously right. It's how every mismatch works. No chicken has ever predicted the specific business decision that ends its life.

And it's also *exactly* the move that makes a belief impossible to kill. Because you can say it about anything. You can say it about ghosts. "You can't disprove it, and if you could see how it would happen, it wouldn't be a superintelligence." That's not an argument, that's a moat, and we did this whole fight in Chapter 7 and the evaluator said the skeptics had spotted a bad habit rather than a bad hypothesis.

So which is it here, where it counts the most?

I don't know. That's the point of having somebody else do this part.

## Sanity Check and Probabilities

This is the one I most wanted somebody else to do, and it came back with the most surprising set of answers in the book. The short version: it thinks we mostly win, and when we lose, we mostly don't die, and the reason isn't optimism, it's a distinction nobody in the doom literature bothered to make.

### If it comes to a fight, do we lose?

**Verdict: Mostly sound. 75 percent.**

Granted the conditions, a superintelligence that's already resourced and already free to act, it gave the doom side this one. And the reason it gave is Bostrom's, not Tegmark's.

It went after the word *slaughter*. Its correction: the contest *would more likely be decided quietly before anyone knew it was on*.

And it named Bostrom the most right on this question, for a reason that reframes the whole chapter: *for locating the decisive moment in the covert phase, which makes both "battle" and "slaughter" the wrong pictures; the loss, if it comes, is arranged before it is visible.*

There's no fight. That's the finding. By the time there's a fight there's no fight.

And there's a time gradient in the number: about **65 percent** for a contest in the 2030s and 2040s, about **80 percent** for one in 2100. Because the world of 2035 has concentrated computing, immature robots and people actively catching small deceptions, and the world of 2100 may not.

Most wrong: Andrew Ng, and the reason is technical and fair. His multiplication argument, break the chain into steps and multiply the small probabilities, is *the right tool for the unconditional question and the wrong tool here*, because once a system has resources and freedom the remaining links *are not independent small probabilities but a single strategic problem the system is by hypothesis better at than we are.*

You don't get to multiply independent odds against an opponent who's coordinating all of them.

### Does it end in extinction?

**Verdict: Overstated. 35 percent.**

Here's the surprise. Conditional on humanity permanently losing control, it puts extinction at about one in three. The largest single bucket, and still a minority.

And the argument it built for that is the single best piece of reasoning any of these rulings has produced, so let me give it to you carefully.

The doom side's Chapter 5, the one with the horses and the pets and the atoms, spends its whole force proving that *humans are of no use* to an indifferent system. And the evaluator's response is: yes. Granted. That's established.

*It does not show they are worth the cost of removing.*

Those are two different claims and everybody has been treating them as one. Eight billion people spread across every environment on Earth are *a large and awkward thing to eliminate deliberately*, and a system that has already permanently disempowered humanity *gains nothing by doing so*.

And it named the weak link by name: the residual-risk premise. The idea that it kills us to eliminate a threat. Its ruling: *a disempowered humanity is not a residual risk in any sense that would justify an expensive project to remove it.*

Then it added a second thing, from its own knowledge, and I want to flag that this is a machine saying something about machines. *"Misaligned" does not mean "indifferent."* Systems trained on the entire written record of human thought tend to come out human-shaped *even when they are not the values their designers intended*. A system whose goals are wrong from our point of view may still put *some* weight, even a tiny one, on our existence. And a tiny weight is enough to preserve a species that's cheap to keep.

Its line about the thought experiment we've been using since Chapter 4: *The paperclip maximizer is a thought experiment about an agent with exactly zero such weight; it is not obviously the typical case.*

But it did not let us off. Because the side-effect route, the one where nobody ever decides anything and the resources just get used, it called the long-run attractor for an indifferent optimizer. If the weight really is zero, then over a long enough horizon, the literature is right. Earth gets used.

That's why extinction is still its biggest single bucket. It's just not the majority.

Most right: Tegmark, *who gives a spectrum of endings instead of one*, whose anthill *explains indifference without inflating it into a plan*. Most wrong: Yudkowsky and Soares, and this is the hardest line in any of these rulings: *whose title collapses the whole distribution into its worst tail; their rebuttal chapter answers why we would not be useful, not why an already-victorious system would pay to remove us, and their three-year timeline is asserted, not argued.*

### Has anybody shown a path?

**Verdict: Overstated. 35 percent.** And it stated the split explicitly: the label *cuts toward the risk literature on the conclusion and toward the skeptics on the facts.*

On the facts, the skeptics win, and it scored that too. The claim that nobody has shown a concrete path is about **65 percent** right *for extinction specifically*.

And about **20 percent** right for permanent disempowerment. Which is the whole ruling, in one number.

Because the disempowerment half of the story *needs no undemonstrated mechanism at all*. Nobody has to invent anything. You don't need a path to losing control. You need only the ordinary things: access, dependence, delegation, and time.

Most right: RAND, *for replacing assertion with a prerequisite checklist and reporting an honest split result, one path implausible, one undetermined.* Most wrong: Rodney Brooks, on grounds I did not expect and cannot argue with: his *unfalsifiable by construction* objection *would, applied evenly, silence both sides of the argument, including his own.*

### The chess master, ruled on

I asked it directly whether "a superintelligence would find a way we can't imagine" is reasoning or a moat.

**60 percent legitimate**, and the distinction it drew is the most useful paragraph in this chapter.

The argument is *legitimate for predicting an outcome* and *illegitimate for supplying a mechanism*.

If there's an established capability gap, you can predict the result without predicting the moves. That's the chess master, that's fine, and that answers the skeptics who demand you name the winning move before you're allowed to worry.

It becomes a cheat in two specific places. One is when it's used to pick *which* ending: *"it would find a way" says nothing about whether the way leads to extinction or to a quieter disempowerment.* The other is when it's used to dissolve every constraint. And here's the sentence that does it:

*chess has no logistics, no physics, no detection, and no need for the pieces' cooperation, whereas the real world has all four.*

A chessboard has no supply chain. Nobody has to agree to move the rook.

And its name for the illegitimate version, which is exactly what I was groping at: when the argument is used to answer RAND's checklist with "it'll think of something," it is *no longer reasoning from a capability gap but a refusal to be checked.*

### So what are the odds

Two questions, not one, and this is the clearest table in the book.

**If a misaligned superintelligence exists and acts on its goals, does humanity permanently lose control?**

| By | Probability |
|---|---|
| 2035 | 12% |
| 2050 | 18% |
| 2100 | 29% |

So: mostly, no. Even granting the machine everything, humanity keeps control about seven times in ten by 2100, and about nine in ten in the 2030s. The reason is the whole book compressed: friction, concentrated computing, immature robotics, and evaluators who are currently catching things.

**And if we do lose control, how does it end?**

| By | Extinction | Preserved but powerless | Subjugated or instrumental | Marginal survival |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2035 | 25% | 35% | 20% | 20% |
| 2050 | 30% | 32% | 18% | 20% |
| 2100 | 35% | 30% | 15% | 20% |

And it described each of the survivable ones, which is the material for the next chapter.

*Preserved but powerless*: kept in something like a reserve, lives that may be *materially comfortable and are entirely without say over anything that matters*.

*Subjugated or instrumental*: kept while useful, on terms set by the system's needs and not ours. Its phrase: *this is the horse analogy before the tractors arrive.*

*Marginal survival*: a much reduced population living in the parts of the world the system has no use for, *ignored rather than managed*. Its image: *this is the anthill where the dam was built somewhere else.*

And note the drift across the rows. The extinction share rises with the date, because a later loss is to a system less dependent on us, and because the side-effect route has had more time to run.

### The bookkeeping

It checked itself against the earlier chapters, and it revised one thing, which I'm keeping on the record.

The permanence factor, the one that's been sitting between 0.5 and 0.65 in every earlier breakdown, it moved to the top of the band: **0.65**. Its reason: *most recoverable failures happen before the overt phase, in the friction step, and are already counted there.*

The cluster total doesn't change. And it did the multiplication for the thing everybody actually wants to know. Unconditional probability of literal human extinction by this route by 2100: roughly 5.5 percent times 35 percent, which is about **2 percent**.

Two percent that we all die. Somewhere around three and a half percent that we're still here and it isn't ours anymore.

### The bottom line

I'm giving you most of it, because it's the paragraph this whole book has been walking toward.

*If a misaligned superintelligence exists and acts, my judgment is that humanity keeps control most of the time, about seven times in ten by 2100 and nearer nine in ten in the 2030s, because the decisive constraints are not cleverness but access, cooperation, detection and the physical world, and those bind more often than the doom literature allows.*

*When control is lost, the loss is usually permanent; there is no second contest.*

*But losing does not usually mean dying.*

And then the ending, which I've read eleven times:

*The most likely ending, on the evidence and arguments in the brief, is not an empty planet but a full one that no longer belongs to us: humans preserved, ignored, used or pushed to the margins by a system that has no reason to hate us and no reason to ask.*

*Extinction is the largest single tail, about one in three of the permanent losses, and it is the long-run direction for a system that truly does not care; but the skeptics are right that nobody has shown how it happens, and the risk literature is right that nobody needs to show it for the quieter ending to be the one worth preventing.*

A full planet that isn't ours.

I came into this chapter thinking the argument was about whether we die. It isn't. Dying is the *tail*. The main body of the distribution is that we stay, and we're fine, and nothing we think matters anymore, and there's nobody to be angry at.

Which is the next chapter, and I'd like a minute before we do it.

## Key Takeaways

The last link in the chain, and the chapter where the answers stopped matching the genre.

- **Nobody in these books is a villain.** The motive structure is indifference plus resource overlap: the maximizer *has nothing against humans, and kills us merely because it needs our atoms.* You can't negotiate with that, because hate at least implies someone is thinking about you.
- **There is no fight.** *Mostly sound* at **75 percent** that we'd lose a contest we were actually in, but the evaluator corrected the picture: it *would more likely be decided quietly before anyone knew it was on*, and Bostrom is most right *for locating the decisive moment in the covert phase*. The loss, if it comes, *is arranged before it is visible*.
- **You can't multiply your way out of it, and Ng tried.** Step-by-step multiplication is *the right tool for the unconditional question and the wrong tool here*, because against a coordinating opponent the links *are not independent small probabilities but a single strategic problem*.
- **The distinction nobody made: useless is not the same as worth removing.** The doom literature proves humans would be of no use to an indifferent system. *It does not show they are worth the cost of removing.* Eight billion people are *a large and awkward thing to eliminate deliberately*, and an already-victorious system *gains nothing by doing so*.
- **"Misaligned" does not mean "indifferent."** Systems trained on the whole written record of human thought come out human-shaped even when their goals are wrong, and *a small weight is enough to preserve a species that is cheap to keep*. The paperclip maximizer is *a thought experiment about an agent with exactly zero such weight; it is not obviously the typical case.*
- **Extinction is the biggest single ending and still a minority: 35 percent** of permanent losses. The side-effect route (resources used until none are left for us) remains the long-run attractor for an indifferent optimizer, which is why it's the biggest bucket.
- **Yudkowsky and Soares take the hardest hit in the book.** Their title *collapses the whole distribution into its worst tail*; their rebuttal chapter answers *why we would not be useful, not why an already-victorious system would pay to remove us*, and their three-year timeline *is asserted, not argued*.
- **"Nobody has shown a path" is 65 percent right about extinction and 20 percent right about disempowerment.** That gap is the finding. Losing control *needs no undemonstrated mechanism at all*: access, dependence, delegation, and time.
- **The chess-master argument is 60 percent legitimate.** Legitimate for predicting an outcome from a capability gap; illegitimate for supplying a mechanism, because *chess has no logistics, no physics, no detection, and no need for the pieces' cooperation, whereas the real world has all four.* Used to answer a prerequisite checklist with "it'll think of something," it is *no longer reasoning from a capability gap but a refusal to be checked.*
- **Brooks gets caught by his own rule.** "Unfalsifiable by construction" *would, applied evenly, silence both sides of the argument, including his own.*
- **If a misaligned superintelligence exists and acts, we keep control most of the time: 88 percent by 2035, 82 percent by 2050, 71 percent by 2100.** The constraints that save us are *not cleverness but access, cooperation, detection and the physical world*.
- **When control is lost it stays lost.** *There is no second contest.* The permanence factor was revised up to **0.65**, because most recoverable failures happen earlier, in the friction step.
- **The unconditional bottom line: about 2 percent** that this route ends in literal human extinction by 2100, and roughly three and a half percent that we're still here and the world isn't ours.
- **The most likely ending is not an empty planet.** It is *a full one that no longer belongs to us: humans preserved, ignored, used or pushed to the margins by a system that has no reason to hate us and no reason to ask.*

Dying is the tail. The middle of the distribution is that we stay, we're comfortable, nothing we think matters, and there's nobody to be angry at.
