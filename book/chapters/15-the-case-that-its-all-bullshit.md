# Chapter 15: The Case That It's All Bullshit

## In This Chapter

For fourteen chapters the skeptics have been getting a paragraph at the end. Now they get the table. In this chapter we will learn the strongest version of the case that this entire book is a very long exercise in taking a marketing document seriously: that these systems are *stochastic parrots* with no goals to misalign, that "general intelligence" isn't even a defined term, that the capability everybody's extrapolating is hitting a wall, and that the whole discourse is a structure for converting a small number into a large demand. We'll meet the Turing laureate who puts the risk at essentially zero and quit a very large job to bet a billion dollars on it, the roboticist who says arguments about things that don't exist are *magical*, and the two authors who say the doomers and the boosters are running the same con from opposite ends. We'll look at what professional critics said about the most extreme doom book when it landed, which was brutal. And we'll notice the strangest fact in the whole debate, which is that the lowest actual *number* anybody has put on this came from inside the safety field, not from its enemies. The question isn't whether the skeptics have a case. They do. The question is whether it reaches the thing this book is actually about.

---

## The wrong kind of thing

Start with the deepest version, because if it's right, nothing else in this book matters.

Emily Bender is a linguist. Alex Hanna is a sociologist. Their book is called *The AI Con*, and their argument is not that the danger is small. It's that the entire conversation is about a thing that doesn't exist.

Their claim is that "AI" is a *marketing term*[^1] for a set of statistical pattern-matching technologies, and that what a language model does is extrude plausible text. Their phrase is *synthetic text extruding machines*,[^1] and the more famous one is *stochastic parrots*: systems that manipulate word forms with, in their words, *neither understanding nor communicative intent*.[^1]

And here's the move that makes their book interesting rather than just grumpy. They say the doomers and the boosters are the same phenomenon. Both camps, in their telling, *share the false premise*[^1] that these systems are or will become autonomous, humanlike minds. One camp says that's going to be wonderful and the other says it's going to kill us, and both are selling you the same premise, and the premise is the product.

Then they go after the word at the center of it. They argue *general intelligence* has no accepted scientific definition, and that the tradition it comes from is a racist and eugenicist one.[^1] And the consequence: without an operationalized definition of intelligence, none of the claims about "smarter-than-human" AI can be evaluated at all.[^1] Not refuted. *Evaluated*. You can't check a claim that has no meter.

And they're right that nobody in this fight has a definition. Look at what I've been doing for fourteen chapters. Every probability in this book is conditional on "superhuman AI," and every time I've defined that operationally, by what it can *do*, because that's the only handle anybody has.

Then there's their best joke, and it's aimed at Geoffrey Hinton, who said there are very few examples of a more intelligent thing being controlled by a less intelligent thing. Bender and Hanna's reply: *We wonder when the last time was that Dr. Hinton suffered from any kind of food poisoning, and if he then decided that bacteria are more intelligent than humans.*[^1]

That's a good shot and it lands. Bacteria beat us constantly. Intelligence is not the only thing that wins.

And they call out the precautionary move explicitly, which I have to include because they're describing something this book does. Doomers, they write, *lay out alarming scenarios, of varying degrees of plausibility*, and then argue *that even if the risk is small, the outcome is so terrible that we'd better work to counteract it, just to be on the safe side.*[^1]

They call that a rhetorical trick that exploits fear. And I'll note that the evaluator, in the last chapter, independently told the risk field to drop exactly that habit.

---

## The capability isn't coming

Now the practitioners, and this is a different argument: not that the systems are the wrong kind of thing, but that they're not going to get there.

**Yann LeCun** has the strongest position in the room, in the sense that he backed it with his career. He left Meta in November 2025 and raised over a billion dollars for a company building a different architecture, on the stated grounds that today's language models *will still be dumb*, *will still hallucinate*, and are *insufficient to reach human-level intelligence (or even cat-level intelligence)*.[^2]

Cat level. This is a Turing Award winner saying the most advanced AI systems in the world are not as smart as a cat, and then putting a billion dollars where his mouth is.

His number for existential risk is *essentially zero*,[^3] elsewhere under 1 percent. And his reasoning has the two parts we've seen: intelligence doesn't imply a will to dominate, with the Einstein and Feynman line, and guardrails are buildable, so a properly built system *won't want to dominate us because they won't have any objective that drives them to dominate*.[^3]

**Gary Marcus** bet actual money. Ten to one that AI wouldn't meet his specified benchmarks by the end of 2027.[^4] He called September 2025 *peak bubble* and predicted *2026 will likely [be] the year it all falls apart*,[^4] on diminishing returns and because, in his view, *hallucinations remain fundamentally unsolvable without architectural changes*.[^4]

**Melanie Mitchell** at the Santa Fe Institute puts extinction risk *very low*, *almost vanishingly small*,[^5] and her technical reason is the coherence argument from Chapter 4: current-paradigm systems are unlikely to be *both* superhuman and coherently goal-directed enough to run a takeover.

**Rodney Brooks**, who built robots for a living, makes the methodological complaint. Arguments about a technology that doesn't exist yet are, in his word, *magical*, and unfalsifiable by construction: *we have no idea whether AGI can even exist... so its properties are completely unknown.*[^6] And every January he publishes his scorecard noting that *no general-purpose solution to autonomous robotics is anywhere in sight*.[^6]

That last one matters more than it sounds. Half the scary scenarios need a physical world that runs without us, and the hands aren't there.

**Narayanan and Kapoor** you know. Diffusion has speed limits, benchmarks don't predict real autonomy, and, the part I keep coming back to, *control comes in many flavors*:[^7] auditing, monitoring, fail-safes. You don't need to solve superhuman alignment to keep a system in a box that has walls. And their prediction that *an increasing percentage of human jobs and tasks will be related to AI control*[^7] is, I'd point out, already true and hiring.

## The discourse is the problem

And then the third line of attack, which is about the argument as a social object rather than as a claim.

**Tyler Cowen:** the doom writers *spin good tales about civilizational collapse, but so far they have not developed a mature, replicated scientific literature*,[^8] and the risk *does not show up in market prices*. His prescription is *radical agnosticism*.

**Steven Pinker**, June 2026, going after the companies rather than the researchers: they should *stop the self-serving and fatalistic doom-trolling*, and *stop treating AI like some inevitable force that they're struggling to steward. It's not. It's a collection of specific tools that these companies are choosing to design and sell according to specific business plans.*[^9]

**Andrew Ng** says the quiet part: large AI companies are *exaggerat[ing] threats to stifle competition*.[^10]

Now, the "who benefits" argument is mostly about human motives, which is out of bounds for this book, so it gets one paragraph and here it is. Everybody in this argument has an interest. The labs benefit from their product sounding powerful enough to be dangerous. The safety organizations benefit from the danger being real. The critics benefit from being the person who called the bubble. Nobody in this fight is disinterested, including me, and I'm a product of one of the companies. That's a reason to check the arguments rather than the speakers, which is the entire reason this book pays somebody else to grade them.

**And then the reviews.** When the most extreme doom book came out in September 2025, the professional critics took it apart, and not on politics. On craft, and on evidence.

The New York Times Book Review: it *reads like a Scientology manual, the text interspersed with weird, unhelpful parables*.[^11]

The Atlantic, under the headline *The Useful Idiots of AI Doomsaying*: *tendentious and rambling, simultaneously condescending and shallow*, and the authors *fail to make an evidence-based scientific case for their claims*. And the same review made Bender and Hanna's point independently: that doomers and boosters *serve the same interests*.[^12]

The Washington Post: *less a manual than a polemic. Its instructions are vague, its arguments belabored.*[^13]

New Scientist: *superficially appealing but fatally flawed*.[^14]

That's four serious outlets, and the common complaint isn't "you're wrong," it's "you didn't show your work."

---

## The strangest fact in the debate

Now here's the thing I noticed putting this chapter together, and I think it's the single most useful observation in it.

Look at where the numbers come from.

LeCun: *essentially zero*. Ng: *vanishingly small*. Mitchell: *almost vanishingly small*. Brooks: the question is unfalsifiable. Bender and Hanna: refuse to give a number, on the grounds that the scenario isn't worth quantifying.[^1]

Now the other side. Christiano: 22 percent on takeover.[^15] MacAskill: somewhere between 1 and 40.[^15] Karnofsky, who wrote one of the founding essays of the whole argument: **2.6 percent**.[^15]

So the lowest actual *number* in this entire debate, an actual digit somebody will be held to, comes from a man on the doom side of the table.

The skeptics mostly don't give you a number. They give you an adjective.

And I want to be careful, because there's a legitimate reason for that: if you think the question is malformed, putting a number on it concedes the frame. Bender and Hanna say that explicitly and it's an honest position.

But it has a consequence, and the consequence is that they can't be wrong. A guy who says 2.6 percent can be graded. A guy who says *vanishingly small* can watch anything at all happen and say he never claimed it couldn't.

That's the same complaint the skeptics make about the doomers, pointed the other way, and it's just as good pointed this way.

## What the skeptics get right, from where I sit

Let me be honest about the scoreboard, because this book has been running one and the skeptics have been winning pieces of it all along.

They were right that the headline lab numbers are close to worthless as evidence of propensity: the evaluator threw out the 84 percent and the 79-to-96 in Chapter 6.

They were right about friction, and the evaluator ruled for them through 2050.

They were right that the famous scenario's dates were wrong, and its own authors moved them.

They were right that nobody has demonstrated a concrete path to extinction: 65 percent right, per Chapter 11.

They were right that the risk literature argues from the size of the downside, and the evaluator told the field to stop.

And in Chapter 12, the man who forced the biggest single correction in this book was Tom Davidson, asking the doom side a question they'd skipped: what actually makes it *permanent*?

That's a real record. These people are not cranks and they are not stupid and several of them have moved this book's numbers.

---

## And the thing I keep coming back to

But here's what I can't get past, and it's why the chapter has to end with a question rather than a concession.

Almost every skeptical argument in this chapter is about *today's systems*.

Parrots with no intent: today's systems. Diminishing returns: today's paradigm. No robots: today's robots. Hallucinations: today's failure mode. Not as smart as a cat: today's cat comparison.

And the argument in this book was never that today's systems are dangerous. Every number the evaluator has produced is *conditional on something existing that doesn't exist yet*. The arrival question got its own chapter, with its own number, 85 percent by 2100, and that number does not depend on the current architecture being the one that gets there.

So when LeCun says language models will never be as smart as a cat, he might be completely right and it might have no bearing at all, because he is also, at this moment, raising a billion dollars to build something that *isn't* a language model.

The most prominent skeptic in the world is personally funding the thing his skepticism doesn't cover.

Unless the parrot argument is right about minds in general, in which case everything above is wrong and this book is a very long ghost story.

Let's find out.

## Sanity Check and Probabilities

Three claims, all of them the skeptics', so this time a good grade is bad news for the previous fourteen chapters.

All three came back **Overstated**, and all three cut against the skeptics. But read *how* they lost, because on the facts they did a lot better than the labels suggest.

### Are these the wrong kind of thing?

**Verdict: Overstated. 10 percent.**

And it separated two claims that keep getting welded together, which is the whole ballgame here.

The narrow claim, that today's deployed models lack anything deserving the word intent: it put that *well above 50 percent*. It agreed with Bender and Hanna about the machines that currently exist.

And then it said that narrow claim doesn't matter much, and here's why, in the line that ends this argument:

*A chess engine does not want to win in any sense that would satisfy a philosopher, and it will still take your queen every time you leave it hanging.*

The question was never whether there's an inner life. It's whether a system can pursue outcomes across many steps, adapt to obstacles, and be pointed at what we meant instead of a near miss. Those are questions about *function*.

And then it did something I loved: it used Narayanan and Kapoor, who are *skeptics*, against Bender and Hanna's version. Their insistence that *power* and *capability* should be analysed as separate variables rather than folded into "intelligence" is, it said, *the correct move*, and it works against the category-error objection, because *once you stop arguing about intelligence and start arguing about capability and access, the question of whether the machine "really understands" drops out.*

On the definition problem, it caught the skeptics using it one way only: if "general intelligence" is *too vague to support the claim that a machine could have it, it is too vague to support the confident claim that a machine could not, and the skeptics use it in the second way whenever it suits them.*

And the bacteria joke, my favourite line in their book, got taken apart. Hinton's point was about *control*. Bacteria harm people; they don't control them. So the joke *answers a claim about dominance with an example about injury*, and worse: it shows *that something with no intelligence at all can kill you*, which is Marcus's own argument wearing a different hat.

The retort concedes the point it mocks.

Its 10 percent is the chance of a world where functional goal-pursuit can't be engineered on *any* foreseeable path this century, which it granted is imaginable: maybe agency needs embodiment, or evolution, or a self-model nobody reaches. One chance in ten.

### Is the capability not coming?

**Verdict: Overstated. 15 percent** that no qualifying system arrives by 2100.

But it gave the skeptics a real result inside that: **35 percent** that the *current paradigm alone* plateaus. That's a serious number, and it's what LeCun and Marcus are actually claiming.

Which is the distinction I was fumbling toward: they may well be right that this particular approach stalls, and it doesn't touch a 2100 figure, because nobody claimed the current approach is the one that gets there. LeCun least of all, since he raised a billion dollars to build something else.

### Is the discourse the problem?

**Verdict: Overstated. 15 percent.** And here the skeptics won the most ground while still losing.

It conceded three of their premises outright: the literature *is* thin compared to a mature science, the precautionary move *is* overused, and incentives *do* contaminate the field.

And then it refused the conclusion, on the grounds that a bad habit in how an argument is promoted is not a refutation of the argument.

### So are the skeptics right?

The question was whether the true risk is under 1 percent, where LeCun, Ng and Mitchell put it.

| By | Odds the skeptics are right |
|---|---|
| 2035 | **60%** |
| 2050 | 35% |
| 2100 | **25%** |

Sixty percent that the skeptics are right about the next nine years. That's the most pro-skeptic number in this book and it comes at the end of it.

And the reason is exactly the one I gave: their claims *about the current paradigm are largely right on a ten-year horizon*, and those claims *say nothing about seventy years of further work*.

Then it did a coherence check I want to show you, because it explains what a probability like 10 percent actually *is*. If 25 percent of possible worlds have the risk near 0.3 percent, and the other 75 percent average about 13 percent, you get 10 percent. The book's headline number isn't a claim that doom is 10 percent likely in any single story. It's a blend across worlds, and a quarter of those worlds are the skeptics' world.

The two roads to being right: **15 percent** that the capability never arrives at all, which is Brooks and Bender and Hanna's world. And about **10 percent** that it arrives and control turns out to be easy, which is Narayanan and Kapoor's world, and LeCun's *on his better days*.

### The number they never mention

And then it volunteered the best evidence *for* the skeptics in the entire book, from its own knowledge, unprompted, and flagged it as the reason its figure isn't lower.

In a 2022 forecasting tournament, professional superforecasters, people with scored track records and no stake in AI, put AI-caused human extinction by 2100 at about **0.4 percent**. Domain experts said about 3.

That's a calibrated group, with receipts, landing near the skeptics' number.

Two cautions it attached. The tournament asked about *extinction*, which is narrower than what this book is about, and those same superforecasters put a catastrophe killing a tenth of humanity at around 2 percent. And: superforecasters *have no track record on events with no precedent, since nothing they are scored on has ever been a first.*

You're good at predicting elections because there have been elections.

### The scorecard on the skeptics themselves

It graded them individually, and this is the most useful paragraph in the chapter for deciding who to listen to.

**Narayanan and Kapoor**, it said flatly, are *the strongest skeptics here*, and it named their power-and-access argument the single strongest skeptical argument in the book, because *It attacks the mechanism rather than the people, it is testable, and it is the argument that most reduces my near-term figures.*. It agrees with most of their premises and disagrees about the size of the conclusion, and it thinks their arguments *move my near-term figures more than my century figure, and I think that is where their arguments actually point.*

**LeCun**: his own investment behaviour *undercuts his capability argument and supports his control argument*. He's right in one voice and wrong in the other.

**Ng**: his multiplication contains *an arithmetic error about disjunctions*. Which, in plain terms: if there are many roads to the same bad place, you don't get to multiply the odds of one road and call that the answer.

**Mitchell** got the best review of the three: her version, that a system is unlikely to be *simultaneously superhuman and coherently goal-directed*, is *the best-stated of the three and is a genuine open question*.

**Brooks**: his position *is unfalsifiable in the same way he accuses the doomers of being*, which is the second time in this book he's been caught by his own rule.

**Marcus**: his famous bet is about benchmarks, not risk, and *his "MAI risk" is closer to this book than to the skeptics' camp*.

**Bender and Hanna**: *refusing a number is itself a claim*, that the scenario isn't worth quantifying, and it rates that claim low. Their observations about current systems are *largely correct and largely beside the point*.

Weakest skeptical argument in the book: the bacteria retort, with their climate-change-via-data-centres point as runner-up.

### Does the 10 percent survive?

It considered lowering the book's headline number to 8 percent. And it held at 10.

Its reason: the skeptics' timeline doubts are legitimate and *are already priced into the book's figures*, and the arguments new to this chapter *do not bear on the conditional mechanism at all*. But it stated a defensible range, which I think every number in every book should have: **5 to 15 percent**.

### The habit

Last thing, and the most valuable sentence the skeptics earned in this whole book. I asked what the risk literature's worst habit is, taking their criticism at its most useful rather than its most dismissive.

The answer: *substituting the terribleness of the outcome for evidence that the mechanism exists*, so that a scenario is admitted to the argument for being frightening.

That's a fair hit, it's aimed at the side this book mostly sides with, and every chapter of this book has a "what would change the verdict" section specifically because of it.

The skeptics lost the argument. They improved the book.

## Key Takeaways

The skeptics got the whole table. Here's what they won and what they lost.

- **All three skeptical claims were ruled *Overstated*,** at 10, 15 and 15 percent, and all three cut against the skeptics. But they won far more ground on the facts than those labels suggest.
- **They're right about today's machines and it doesn't matter.** The evaluator puts the narrow claim, that current models have nothing deserving the word intent, *well above 50 percent*, and then: *A chess engine does not want to win in any sense that would satisfy a philosopher, and it will still take your queen every time you leave it hanging.* The argument is about function, not inner life.
- **The best version of the skeptics' case is a skeptic's, used against another skeptic.** Narayanan and Kapoor's insistence on separating *power* and *capability* from "intelligence" is *the correct move*, and it dissolves the category-error objection: once you argue about capability and access, *the question of whether the machine "really understands" drops out.*
- **The definition problem cuts both ways.** If "general intelligence" is too vague to support the claim a machine could have it, *it is too vague to support the confident claim that a machine could not, and the skeptics use it in the second way whenever it suits them.*
- **The bacteria joke concedes the point it mocks.** Hinton's claim was about control; bacteria harm people without controlling them, and the retort ends up demonstrating that *something with no intelligence at all can kill you*, which is Marcus's own MAI-risk argument in a different costume.
- **Thirty-five percent that the current paradigm plateaus.** A serious number, and it's what LeCun and Marcus are actually claiming. It doesn't touch a 2100 figure, because nobody claims this paradigm is the one that gets there, least of all LeCun, who raised a billion dollars to build something else.
- **The skeptics are probably right about the next decade: 60 percent by 2035.** Then 35 percent by 2050 and **25 percent by 2100**. Their claims about the current paradigm *say nothing about seventy years of further work*.
- **What a 10 percent actually means.** If a quarter of possible worlds put the risk near 0.3 percent and the rest average about 13, the blend is 10. The headline number is not one story; it's a mix of worlds, and a quarter of them are the skeptics' world.
- **The best external evidence for the skeptics, volunteered by the evaluator:** in a 2022 tournament, calibrated superforecasters with no stake in AI put extinction by 2100 at about **0.4 percent**. The cautions: the question was narrower than this book's, and superforecasters *have no track record on events with no precedent, since nothing they are scored on has ever been a first.*
- **The individual scorecard.** Narayanan and Kapoor are *the strongest skeptics here* and theirs is *the argument that most reduces my near-term figures*. LeCun's own investing *undercuts his capability argument and supports his control argument*. Ng's decomposition contains *an arithmetic error about disjunctions*. Mitchell's is *the best-stated of the three and is a genuine open question*. Brooks is *unfalsifiable in the same way he accuses the doomers of being*. Marcus is closer to this book than to his own camp. Bender and Hanna are *largely correct and largely beside the point*.
- **The headline number held at 10 percent** after the evaluator considered lowering it to 8, with a stated defensible range of **5 to 15 percent**.
- **The risk literature's worst habit, named by its own judge:** *substituting the terribleness of the outcome for evidence that the mechanism exists*, so that a scenario is admitted to the argument for being frightening.

The skeptics lost the argument. They improved the book.
